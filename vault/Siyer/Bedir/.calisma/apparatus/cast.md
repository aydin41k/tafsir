# APPARATUS: CAST, CASUALTIES, GLOSSARY, CHRONOLOGY

*Reference matter for the chapter on Badr. Clan affiliations follow Ibn Ishaq's rosters of the men of Badr as
preserved by Ibn Hisham — the lists of participants, of the Muslim dead, and of the Quraysh killed and captured,
which stand together near the end of his account of the battle (Guillaume, The Life of Muhammad) — checked
against al-Waqidi's Kitab al-Maghazi, book of Badr, and against the biographical entries in Ibn Sa'd's Tabaqat,
where a man's clan and his death-date can be looked up independently of any storyteller.*

*A word on how to read the clan names. Every Meccan here belonged to a clan of Quraysh (Banu Hashim, Banu
Makhzum, Banu 'Abd Shams and so on), and among the Ansar of Medina every man belonged to a clan of either Aws or
Khazraj; both bloc and clan are given. Where a man was a sworn confederate (halif) or a client or freedman
(mawla) of a clan rather than born into it, that is said instead, because at Badr it frequently mattered more
than blood: it decided who marched, who was spared, and who was ransomed by whom.*

*Where the sources disagree the disagreement is flagged rather than hidden. A dagger (†) marks an attribution
the sources contest; in the tables of the dead it appears against the killer's name, and a multiplied dagger
against Abu Jahl's entry marks the most tangled attribution of the day. Dates follow the chapter: the battle on
Friday 17 Ramadan 2 AH, 13 March 624 CE.*

## 1. THE PRINCIPAL FIGURES

### The Muslims

**The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ** — Banu Hashim of Quraysh. Commanded in person, chose the ground on the near bank,
dressed the ranks with an arrow-shaft, and prayed in the 'arish with Abu Bakr beside him. Forbade the killing
of al-'Abbas and of the Hashimites who had been dragged out unwilling. Died at Medina in Rabi' I 11 AH / June
632, eight years after Badr.

**Abu Bakr al-Siddiq** ('Abd Allah ibn Abi Quhafa) — Banu Taym. Muhajir. Sat with the Prophet in the shelter
and put the fallen cloak back on his shoulders; at the council on the prisoners he argued for ransom. His own
son 'Abd al-Rahman stood in the Quraysh line and did not convert until later. First caliph; died 13 AH / 634.

**'Umar ibn al-Khattab** — Banu 'Adi. Muhajir, in the ranks. Killed his own maternal uncle al-'As ibn Hisham
ibn al-Mughira. Argued for striking off the prisoners' heads and lost the argument. Second caliph; assassinated
at Medina, 23 AH / 644.

**'Ali ibn Abi Talib** — Banu Hashim. Muhajir, about twenty-three, one of the three who answered the challenge
to single combat and the only one to walk away from it; killed al-Walid ibn 'Utba, then Nawfal ibn Khuwaylid
and others. Carried the Prophet's black banner, al-'Uqab, by most reports. Fourth caliph; assassinated at
Kufa, 40 AH / 661.

**Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib** — Banu Hashim. The Prophet's uncle, and the most conspicuous man on the field:
he wore an ostrich feather in his helmet so that he could be picked out. Killed Shayba ibn Rabi'a in the duel,
al-Aswad ibn 'Abd al-Asad before it, and Tu'ayma ibn 'Adi after. Killed at Uhud, 3 AH / 625.

**'Ubayda ibn al-Harith** — Banu al-Muttalib. Muhajir, the oldest of the three champions; the reports make him
something over sixty, and a kinsman of the Prophet's, which is why 'Utba accepted him as a match — the pairing
was by age and standing rather than by strength. Fought
'Utba ibn Rabi'a, wounded him mortally, and lost his own leg to the same exchange; carried off the field on a
cloak. Died of the wound at al-Safra' on the march home, the last of the fourteen to die and the only one who
did not die in the valley.

**al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam** — Banu Asad ibn 'Abd al-'Uzza. Muhajir; one of only two mounted men in the army,
recognisable by a yellow turban. Ran his spear through the eye-slit of 'Ubayda ibn Sa'id ibn al-'As's visor,
the one gap in a suit of armour that covered everything else. Killed in 36 AH / 656 after the Battle of the
Camel.

**Mus'ab ibn 'Umayr** — Banu 'Abd al-Dar. Muhajir; carried the white liwa'. Took his own full brother Abu
'Aziz prisoner and told the man holding him to tie the ropes tighter, because their mother in Mecca was rich
and would pay. Killed at Uhud carrying the same standard.

**'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf** — Banu Zuhra. Muhajir, in the ranks. Tried to bring Umayya ibn Khalaf and his son
out of the mêlée alive, on the strength of an old friendship, and watched the Ansar kill them both in front of
him. Became the wealthiest of the Companions; died 32 AH / 652.

**Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas** — Banu Zuhra. Muhajir; an archer, and one of the younger men in the ranks — the reports
of his age at conversion put him in his late twenties at Badr. His younger brother 'Umayr
was among the dead. Later conquered Iraq and founded Kufa; died at Medina about 55 AH / 675, one of the last
survivors of Badr.

**Abu 'Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah** — Banu al-Harith ibn Fihr. Muhajir, in mail in the centre. His father al-Jarrah
was in the opposing line; the reports say he avoided him as long as he could and then killed him. Commanded in
Syria; died in the plague of 'Amwas, 18 AH / 639.

**Abu Hudhayfa ibn 'Utba** — Banu 'Abd Shams. Muhajir. His father 'Utba, his uncle Shayba and his brother
al-Walid were all killed at Badr, all three in the opening duel; he was seen weeping as their bodies were
dragged to the pit and the Prophet spoke to him gently. Killed at al-Yamama, 12 AH / 633.

**'Abdullah ibn Mas'ud** — of Hudhayl, a client of Banu Zuhra. Muhajir; a small, slight man. Scouted the
Quraysh camp with 'Ammar ibn Yasir on the night before. Found Abu Jahl down but not dead, finished him and cut
off his head. Later the great Qur'an teacher of Kufa; died 32 AH / 653.

**al-Miqdad ibn 'Amr al-Aswad** — of Bahra', confederate of Banu Zuhra. Muhajir; the second of the two
horsemen. Had spoken for the march at the council in Medina. Died 33 AH / 653. One minority report makes him,
not 'Ali, the executioner of al-Nadr ibn al-Harith.†

**Bilal ibn Rabah** — Abyssinian, freedman of Abu Bakr. Muhajir. Recognised Umayya ibn Khalaf, who had owned
and tortured him in Mecca, being led away as a prisoner, and shouted until the Ansar cut him down. The
Prophet's mu'adhdhin; died at Damascus about 20 AH / 641.

**'Ammar ibn Yasir** — client of Banu Makhzum. Muhajir; sent with Ibn Mas'ud to look over the Quraysh camp in
the dark. Killed at Siffin, 37 AH / 657, fighting for 'Ali.

**Zayd ibn Haritha** — freedman and adopted son of the Prophet. Muhajir. Rode ahead from al-Rawha' on the
Prophet's own she-camel al-Qaswa' to carry the news of the victory into Medina. Commanded at Mu'ta and was
killed there, 8 AH / 629.

**'Ukkasha ibn Mihsan** — Banu Asad ibn Khuzayma, an ally. Muhajir; fought on with a wooden staff after his
sword broke, which is the detail that best conveys how few weapons the army had. Killed in the Ridda wars at
Buzakha by Tulayha ibn Khuwaylid, 12 AH / 633.

**Sa'd ibn Mu'adh** — Aws, Banu 'Abd al-Ashhal; chief of his clan. Spoke for the Ansar at the council before
the march — "if you took us into the sea we would plunge in with you" — and proposed and commanded the guard
on the 'arish. Took an arrow at the siege of the Trench in 5 AH and died of the wound.

**al-Hubab ibn al-Mundhir** — Khazraj, Banu Salima. Asked the Prophet whether the first halting-place was
revelation or his own judgement, and on being told it was judgement, urged pushing on to the nearest well of
Badr and wrecking the others. The advice gave the Muslims the water. Died in 'Umar's caliphate.

**Mu'adh ibn 'Amr ibn al-Jamuh** — Khazraj, Banu Salima. Cut Abu Jahl's leg from under him — the wound that
put him on the ground. 'Ikrima ibn Abi Jahl nearly took his arm off in return; he fought on with it hanging
and finally tore it away and threw it aside. Lived into 'Uthman's reign.

**'Awf ibn al-Harith ibn 'Afra'** — Khazraj, Banu al-Najjar. Asked the Prophet what makes God laugh with
delight at a servant, was told, a man plunging into the enemy without mail, and stripped off his coat and did
it. Killed at Badr, most reports say by Abu Jahl.

**Mu'awwidh ibn al-Harith ibn 'Afra'** — Khazraj, Banu al-Najjar. Brother of 'Awf. Struck at Abu Jahl where he
lay and was himself killed soon after. The two brothers are the reason the sources speak of the sons of 'Afra'
in the same breath as Abu Jahl's death.

**Abu Dujana Simak ibn Kharasha** — Khazraj, Banu Sa'ida. In the line at Badr; the red headband and the
Prophet's sword belong to Uhud, not here. Killed at al-Yamama, 12 AH / 633, by most reports.

**'Abdullah ibn Rawaha** — Khazraj, Banu al-Harith. In the ranks; sent with Zayd as the second herald of the
victory. At the council on the prisoners he sided with 'Umar for execution. Killed at Mu'ta, 8 AH / 629.

**al-Mujadhdhir ibn Dhiyad al-Balawi** — of Balī, confederate of the Aws. Killed Abu'l-Bakhtari against his
own wish, after offering him quarter twice. Killed at Uhud by al-Harith ibn Suwayd, in payment for a
pre-Islamic blood debt.

**Khubayb ibn Isaf** — Khazraj, Banu al-Harith. Killed al-Harith ibn 'Amir ibn Nawfal. He had come out to join
the march while still a pagan and was sent back until he accepted Islam.

**Abu'l-Yasar Ka'b ibn 'Amr** — Khazraj, Banu Salima. A short man, and the captor of al-'Abbas, which was
thought remarkable enough that the sources argue about how he managed it. Also killed Munabbih ibn al-Hajjaj.
Died about 55 AH.

**'Asim ibn Thabit** — Aws, Banu 'Amr ibn 'Awf. An archer in the Aws line, and by the better-attested account
the man who put 'Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt to death at 'Irq al-Zabya on the road home.† Killed at al-Raji', 4 AH.

**Abu Lubaba ibn 'Abd al-Mundhir** — Aws, Banu 'Amr ibn 'Awf. Turned back at al-Rawha' to hold Medina, and was
counted among the men of Badr in the division of the spoils although he never saw the field. His brother
Mubashshir was killed there.

### The Quraysh

**Abu Jahl** ('Amr ibn Hisham) — Banu Makhzum. Not the nominal commander but the effective one, since he broke
every attempt to call the campaign off; held the centre with his own clan, the largest contingent on the field.
Hamstrung by Mu'adh ibn 'Amr, struck by Mu'awwidh ibn 'Afra', and finished on the ground by 'Abdullah ibn
Mas'ud, who took his head.† Thrown into the qalib.

**'Utba ibn Rabi'a** — Banu 'Abd Shams. The senior man of Quraysh present and the one who did not want the
battle: he rode the front on his red camel urging withdrawal and offered to pay the blood-money for 'Amr
al-Hadrami out of his own purse. Taunted as a coward by Abu Jahl, he walked out to the duel, could find no
helmet large enough for his head, and bound a cloth round it instead. Mortally wounded by 'Ubayda ibn
al-Harith. Thrown into the qalib.

**Shayba ibn Rabi'a** — Banu 'Abd Shams. Brother of 'Utba; the second of the three champions. Killed by Hamza.
Thrown into the qalib.

**al-Walid ibn 'Utba** — Banu 'Abd Shams. Son of 'Utba, the youngest of the three champions and the first of
them to fall, killed by 'Ali. His brother Abu Hudhayfa was in the Muslim line. Thrown into the qalib.

**Umayya ibn Khalaf** — Banu Jumah. A rich and reluctant man who had to be shamed into marching, and Bilal's
former owner. Taken alive under 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf's protection and killed anyway, with his son 'Ali ibn
Umayya, when Bilal recognised him. Thrown into the qalib.

**Abu Sufyan ibn Harb** — Banu 'Abd Shams. Master of the caravan, and the reason for everything that followed:
he sent Damdam ibn 'Amr to Mecca for help, then swung the column west to the coast road and brought it home
untouched — and so was not at the battle at all. His son Hanzala was killed there and another son, 'Amr, taken
prisoner. Later became a Muslim at the conquest of Mecca; died about 32 AH / 653.

**al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib** — Banu Hashim. The Prophet's uncle, in the Quraysh ranks. Taken prisoner by
Abu'l-Yasar and bound so tightly that his groaning kept the Prophet from sleeping until the ropes were eased.
Assessed the heaviest ransom of the day: for himself, his two nephews and his ally. Whether he came out under
compulsion or willingly is the single most argued question in the roster.† Later a Muslim; died about 32 AH.

**'Aqil ibn Abi Talib** — Banu Hashim. Brother of 'Ali, forced out with the army; taken prisoner and ransomed
by al-'Abbas. Later a Muslim.

**Talib ibn Abi Talib** — Banu Hashim. Eldest brother of 'Ali, dragged out with the Quraysh. He was neither
among the dead nor among the prisoners, and nothing further is known of him — a gap the sources do not fill.

**'Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt** — Banu 'Abd Shams. Among the loudest for battle; in Mecca he had been the man who
threw filth at the Prophet and once tried to strangle him at prayer. Taken alive and put to death on the
return march at 'Irq al-Zabya, by 'Asim ibn Thabit in the better-attested version, by 'Ali in others.†

**al-Nadr ibn al-Harith** — Banu 'Abd al-Dar. Standard-bearer of Quraysh — his clan held the standard by
hereditary right — and in Mecca the man who used to follow the Prophet's recitations with Persian romances to
draw the crowd away. Taken alive; executed at al-Safra' on the road home, by 'Ali in most reports.†

**Suhayl ibn 'Amr** — Banu 'Amir ibn Lu'ayy. Leader of his clan's contingent, and the orator of Quraysh. Taken
prisoner; 'Umar asked leave to pull out his front teeth so he could never speak against Islam again and was
refused. Ransomed at four thousand dirhams. Became a Muslim at the conquest, and it was his speech that kept
Mecca calm on the day the Prophet died.

**Hakim ibn Hizam** — Banu Asad ibn 'Abd al-'Uzza. Nephew of Khadija, and 'Utba's messenger in the attempt to
turn the army round; carried the proposal to the chiefs and then to Abu Jahl, who was oiling his mail and
refused. Escaped the field, became a Muslim at the conquest, and afterwards would not pass the site of Badr
without hurrying his mount.

**'Umayr ibn Wahb al-Jumahi** — Banu Jumah. A hard-bitten raider, sent out on the morning of the battle to ride
round the Muslim position: he came back with "three hundred men, a few more or a few less," rode out again to
look for hidden supports, and reported none — and then warned the army that these were men with nothing to
lose. His son Wahb was taken prisoner. Later a Muslim.

**'Ikrima ibn Abi Jahl** — Banu Makhzum. Fought beside his father; nearly severed Mu'adh ibn 'Amr's arm and
killed Rafi' ibn al-Mu'alla. Fled the field. Became a Muslim after the conquest of Mecca and was killed
fighting the Byzantines in Syria.

**'Amir ibn al-Hadrami** — a Hadrami client of Banu 'Abd Shams. Brother of the 'Amr killed at Nakhla the year
before, whose blood was the standing pretext for the campaign. Put up by Abu Jahl to raise the feud-cry — *wa
'Amra!* — which killed 'Utba's peace proposal on the spot. Credited with the arrow that killed Mihja'. Killed
in the mêlée.

**Abu'l-Bakhtari** (al-'As ibn Hisham) — Banu Asad ibn 'Abd al-'Uzza. One of the Meccans who had worked to
break the boycott of Banu Hashim, and one of those the Prophet would rather not have had killed. Offered
quarter by al-Mujadhdhir and refused to take it while his companion was cut down beside him; killed. Thrown
into the qalib.

**al-Aswad ibn 'Abd al-Asad al-Makhzumi** — Banu Makhzum. Swore he would drink from the Muslims' cistern or
destroy it or die at it, and went forward alone to try; killed by Hamza — the first man of either side to fall.
Thrown into the qalib.

**Tu'ayma ibn 'Adi ibn Nawfal** — Banu Nawfal. Killed by Hamza. His nephew Jubayr ibn Mut'im would later put
Wahshi up to killing Hamza at Uhud in revenge. Thrown into the qalib.

**al-Akhnas ibn Shariq** — of Thaqif, confederate of Banu Zuhra. Turned the whole Zuhra contingent round at
al-Juhfa and marched it home, which is why not one man of Banu Zuhra was killed or taken at Badr. He never
became a Muslim.

**Munabbih ibn al-Hajjaj** — Banu Sahm. Joint leader of his clan's contingent with his brother. Killed by
Abu'l-Yasar Ka'b ibn 'Amr and the Ansar. Thrown into the qalib.

**Nubayh ibn al-Hajjaj** — Banu Sahm. Brother of Munabbih; killed by Abu Usayd Malik ibn Rabi'a and the Ansar.
Thrown into the qalib.

**Abu'l-'As ibn al-Rabi'** — Banu 'Abd Shams. The Prophet's son-in-law, husband of his daughter Zaynab; taken
prisoner, ransomed by his brother, and released on an undertaking to let Zaynab go to Medina, which he kept.
Became a Muslim years later.

**al-Walid ibn al-Walid ibn al-Mughira** — Banu Makhzum. Brother of Khalid ibn al-Walid; ransomed at four
thousand dirhams by his brothers, then became a Muslim immediately after and had to escape Mecca a second time.

**Suhayl ibn Bayda'** — Banu al-Harith ibn Fihr. Already a Muslim in secret and forced out with Quraysh;
released without ransom when 'Abdullah ibn Mas'ud testified that he had prayed with them in Mecca.

## 2. THE FOURTEEN

The Muslim dead. The number is fourteen in Ibn Ishaq and in al-Waqidi, and the division into six Muhajirun and
eight Ansar is standard; a handful of later lists give eight and eight, or six and eight with 'Ubayda ibn
al-Harith counted out because he died on the road rather than on the field. I keep him in, and keep the total at
fourteen.

### Muhajirun (6)

|---|---|---|
| **'Ubayda ibn al-Harith** | Banu al-Muttalib | Leg severed by **'Utba ibn Rabi'a** in the opening single combat; died of the wound at **al-Safra'** on the return march and was buried there. The oldest of the fourteen. |
| **'Umayr ibn Abi Waqqas** | Banu Zuhra | About sixteen, younger brother of Sa'd; killed in the mêlée. Killer not named. |
| **Dhu al-Shimalayn** — 'Umayr ibn 'Abd 'Amr | Khuza'a, confederate of Banu Zuhra | Killed in the fighting in the centre. Killer not named. His nickname means "the man of two left hands", or of two right hands, depending on the report. |
| **'Aqil ibn al-Bukayr** | of Banu Sa'd ibn Layth, ally of Banu 'Adi | Killed by **Malik ibn Zuhayr al-Jushami**. |
| **Mihja'**, freedman of 'Umar ibn al-Khattab | client of Banu 'Adi | The first Muslim killed at Badr, hit by an arrow while the ranks still stood, before the lines closed; the shot attributed to **'Amir ibn al-Hadrami**.† |
| **Safwan ibn Bayda'** | Banu al-Harith ibn Fihr | Killed in the mêlée. Killer not named. Brother of the Suhayl ibn Bayda' who was on the other side and released without ransom. |

Two things are worth noticing about this list. The first is how young and how ordinary most of it is: one
sixteen-year-old, two brothers of the same mother, a freed slave, a man shot while drinking. The second is the
distribution. Six of the eight Ansar dead are Khazraj, which matches the Khazraj's larger share of the army;
and thirteen of the fourteen fell on the field, in a battle that lasted a few hours of one morning.

## 3. THE WELL

After the fighting the Prophet ordered the Quraysh dead gathered and put into a dry well — a *qalib* — in the
valley floor, and the mouth of it filled in. The sources name about twenty-four men thrown in, all of them of
the chiefs and the notables; the remaining Quraysh dead were buried where they lay or left to their own people.
Ibn Ishaq preserves the fullest list of names, al-Waqidi the fullest list of killers, and the two do not always
agree. A dagger (†) marks an attribution the sources dispute — and a good many of them are disputed, because
claiming a chief's death was a matter of clan honour for generations afterwards.

| **'Umayr ibn 'Uthman ibn Ka'b** | Taym | **Salit ibn Qays** and the Khazraj.† |

Ibn Ishaq reports that the Prophet stood at the mouth of the pit as the light went and called down into it by
name — "'Utba ibn Rabi'a, Shayba ibn Rabi'a, Umayya ibn Khalaf, Abu Jahl ibn Hisham" — and asked whether they
had found what God and His Messenger promised them true, "for I have found what God promised me true." 'Umar
asked what use there was in speaking to bodies with no life in them, and was told that they heard what he said
no better than 'Umar did, but they could not answer.

Two more Quraysh notables died at the Prophet's order on the road home rather than in the valley, and so are
not in the well: **al-Nadr ibn al-Harith** of 'Abd al-Dar, executed at al-Safra' — by 'Ali in the majority of
reports, by al-Miqdad in others† — and **'Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt** of 'Abd Shams, executed at 'Irq al-Zabya by
'Asim ibn Thabit al-Ansari in the better-attested version, by 'Ali in a competing one.†

## 4. GLOSSARY

**al-** — the Arabic definite article, "the", prefixed to nouns and to many names: *al-Zubayr*, *al-'Abbas*,
*al-Hubab*. It is part of the name and does not affect alphabetisation in this book. In speech the *l* is often
swallowed into the following consonant, so *al-Nadr* is said *an-Nadr* and *al-Safra'* is said *as-Safra'*.

**Ansar** — "the Helpers": the Muslims of Medina, natives of the oasis, who had invited the Prophet north and
who housed and fed the emigrants from Mecca. At Badr they made up something over two-thirds of the army, and
their willingness to fight a battle outside their own territory — which the original agreement had not required
of them — was the question the council before the march existed to settle. They fell into two large tribal
blocs, Aws and Khazraj.

**'arish** — a shelter of branches, palm-fronds and cloth, thrown up in an hour. At Badr one was built for the
Prophet on a rise behind the Muslim right, on Sa'd ibn Mu'adh's suggestion, with a guard on it and a camel kept
saddled beside it. It was not a command post in any modern sense; it was shade, and a place to pray from.

**Aws** — one of the two great Arab tribal blocs of Medina, the smaller of the two, settled mainly in the
southern and eastern quarters of the oasis. Sa'd ibn Mu'adh's Banu 'Abd al-Ashhal were Aws. Two of the fourteen
Muslim dead at Badr were Aws.

**diya** — blood-money: the compensation in camels or silver that a killer's kin paid to the dead man's kin to
close a feud without further killing. It was the mechanism that kept Arabian society from bleeding itself out,
and it is why 'Utba ibn Rabi'a's offer to pay the diya for 'Amr al-Hadrami out of his own wealth was a serious
proposal and not a gesture: it would have removed the campaign's legal pretext at a stroke.

**fidya** — ransom. At Badr the ransoms for the seventy prisoners were assessed between one thousand and four
thousand dirhams a head according to a man's wealth and standing; prisoners too poor to pay but able to write
were released for teaching ten children of Medina their letters, and some were released for nothing.

**ghanima** — the spoils of a raid or a battle: weapons, mail, mounts, cloth, silver, and the prisoners.
Pre-Islamic custom gave the taker what he took. The disputes over the division at Badr, and the ruling that
followed in Surat al-Anfal — a fifth set aside and the rest shared out — mark the point at which a tribal
practice became a state's revenue.

**hawd** — a cistern or watering-trough. Al-Hubab ibn al-Mundhir's advice was to seize the nearest well of Badr
and build a hawd at it, so that the whole army could drink from a standing reservoir rather than queue at the
well-head. Two of the day's deaths happened at it.

**hijra** — the emigration of 622, when the Prophet and some seventy Meccan households left Mecca for Medina.
The Muslim calendar counts its years from it: 2 AH, the year of Badr, is the second year after the hijra. Those
who made the move are the **Muhajirun**.

**ibn / bint** — "son of" and "daughter of"; *ibn* is often abbreviated *b.* in scholarly writing. 'Utba ibn
Rabi'a is 'Utba, son of Rabi'a, and 'Atika bint 'Abd al-Muttalib is 'Atika, daughter of 'Abd al-Muttalib. A chain
of them gives a pedigree, so al-'As ibn Hisham ibn al-Mughira is al-'As, son of Hisham, son of al-Mughira.
Arabic names carry no fixed surname, and a man is identified by his father, his clan, or his **kunya** — which is
why two different men at Badr can both be called al-'As ibn Hisham and be no relation.

**isnad** — the chain of transmitters attached to a report, naming who heard it from whom back to an
eyewitness. It is the central instrument of Muslim historical criticism and it does real work: it lets the
sources be graded, and it makes wholesale invention difficult. It does not eliminate embellishment, since a
sound chain can carry a story that grew in the telling, and it says nothing about a transmitter's memory for
numbers.

**Khazraj** — the larger of Medina's two Arab tribal blocs, settled mainly in the central and western quarters of
the oasis and subdivided into clans of its own, of which Banu al-Najjar, Banu Salima, Banu Zurayq and Banu
Sa'ida all sent men to Badr. Something like 170 of the 313 were Khazraj — more than half the army — and six of
the fourteen Muslim dead. The Aws and the Khazraj had fought each other for generations, most recently and most
bloodily at Bu'ath five years before the hijra; that they stood in one line at Badr was itself recent and
fragile, and the sources let the old rivalry show in the competition over who had done most on the day.

**kunya** — the by-name formed with *Abu*, "father of", or *Umm*, "mother of": Abu Bakr, Abu Sufyan, Abu Jahl.
Most were affectionate or descriptive rather than literal. *Abu Jahl*, "father of ignorance", is not one of
these: it is a Muslim nickname that stuck so hard it displaced the man's real name, 'Amr ibn Hisham, from the
record almost entirely.

**liwa' / raya** — the two kinds of standard. The *liwa'* was the army's single principal banner, tied to a
lance-head and carried near the commander; the *raya* was a smaller flag borne by a contingent. At Badr Mus'ab
ibn 'Umayr carried the white liwa'; there was a black raya with 'Ali and another with the Ansar. Losing a
standard was a public disaster, and the men who carried them were expected to die before dropping them.

**mail, coat of mail** (*dir'*) — a shirt of interlinked iron rings, sometimes with a hood and sleeves, worn
over padding. It was the single most expensive thing a fighting man could own, worth many camels, and it was
very effective against a cut and only fair against a thrust. The Muslims at Badr had six between three hundred
and thirteen men; a good part of the Quraysh nobility was in mail from the throat down, which is why the
sources make so much of finding the gaps.

**mala'** — the assembly of the chiefs of Quraysh: the heads of the clans who met in the courtyard beside the
Ka'ba and decided Mecca's affairs by consensus, having no king and no standing army. The Qur'an and the sira
both use the word for the men who ran Mecca, and Badr took most of them off the board in a single morning.

**maghazi** — the genre of Arabic writing devoted to the Prophet's expeditions, campaign by campaign;
al-Waqidi's *Kitab al-Maghazi* is the classic of it. The maghazi are richer in circumstantial detail than the
general biographies and correspondingly more suspect for it: the detail had a century and more in which to
accumulate.

**mubaraza** — single combat before the general engagement. Champions stepped out of the line, named
themselves, and fought in view of both armies. It was a real form of battle and not a formality: it could
settle the day's temper, and at Badr it did, since all three Quraysh champions were of one family and all three
were killed.

**Muhajirun** — "the Emigrants": the Meccan Muslims who had left their city, their clans and mostly their
property for Medina. At Badr they numbered somewhere between seventy-four and eighty-six of the army, and they
were fighting their own fathers, brothers and uncles, which the sources never let the reader forget.

**qalib** — a dry or disused well: a shaft, sometimes lined, going down to a level the water has left. The
Quraysh dead at Badr were put into one and its mouth filled in.

**Quraysh** — the tribe that held Mecca and its sanctuary, divided into a dozen or so clans — Hashim, 'Abd
Shams, Makhzum, Jumah, Sahm, Nawfal, 'Abd al-Dar, Asad, Taym, 'Adi, Zuhra, 'Amir — which competed, intermarried
and quarrelled and which acted together only when they had to. The Prophet was Qurayshi, of Banu Hashim; so was
almost every man in the line opposite him. Badr was in that sense a family quarrel fought with weapons.

**Ramadan** — the ninth month of the Muslim year, in which the fast from dawn to sunset is kept. Badr was
fought on the seventeenth of it, in a valley, in March, by men who had marched eighty miles to get there. The
sources report that the Prophet permitted the fast to be broken on the march, and that some kept it anyway.

**sira** — the biography of the Prophet as a literary form. Ibn Ishaq's *Sirat Rasul Allah*, which survives
through Ibn Hisham's edited recension, is the foundation of the genre and the oldest continuous narrative we
have of Badr.

**sunna** — the Prophet's practice: what he did, said and permitted, taken by Muslims as normative alongside
the Qur'an. It matters to the sources' shape, because a report about Badr was very often preserved not for its
historical interest but because a legal ruling hung on it — the division of spoils, the treatment of prisoners,
prayer on campaign.

**wadi** — a valley cut by water that runs only after rain: dry sand or gravel most of the year, a torrent for a
few hours after a storm, with a water table close enough beneath the surface for wells and palms. Badr sits in
one. The wadi's two banks, the soft sand on the Quraysh side and the firmer ground on the Muslim side, decided
much of the battle.

## 5. CHRONOLOGY

The anchor is 17 Ramadan 2 AH, converted by the standard tables to 13 March 624 CE. Everything else here is
counted forward and back from that day, and the arithmetic is the table's own, not the sources'.

The conversion needs one caveat stated plainly. The sources are unanimous that Badr was fought on a Friday, and
13 March 624 in the Julian calendar — which is the calendar the Near East actually kept in 624 — was a Tuesday.
The mismatch is not a slip by anybody. Conversion tables run the Muslim calendar arithmetically, on alternating
months of thirty and twenty-nine days from a fixed epoch, whereas the calendar of 624 was observational: each
month began when a man saw the new crescent, and an overcast evening at Mecca could push a month a day late. Two
or three days of accumulated drift over two years is entirely ordinary, and would put 17 Ramadan on Julian 16
March, a Friday. I keep 13 March because it is the figure the whole literature uses, and note that the true date
was probably a day or three later. Readers who see "17 March 624" elsewhere are looking at the same day expressed
in proleptic Gregorian reckoning, which did not exist yet.

Two warnings. First, the caravan's departure from Gaza is nowhere dated. A thousand camels moving south down
the incense road at the thirty to thirty-five kilometres a day such a column could sustain would need five or
six weeks for the journey, which puts Abu Sufyan out of Gaza in the first half of Sha'ban, a month before the
battle; the entries before 1 Ramadan are therefore reconstruction and are marked as such. Second, the date of
the march out of Medina is itself disputed: one tradition gives 8 Ramadan, another 12 Ramadan, and Ibn Sa'd
preserves both. This table follows 12 Ramadan, with the chapter. It is the more widely reported figure, and the
distance does not argue against it: Badr is about eighty miles from Medina by the track, or a little over a
hundred and thirty kilometres, which is five days at the twenty-five to thirty-five kilometres a day a column
of men mostly on foot could keep up. The 8 Ramadan reckoning would leave nine days for that march, which is
slow rather than impossible, and it has the further difficulty of putting the army at Badr several days before
Quraysh could have got there. Where the two schemes differ, the events are fixed by their sequence and by the
one certain date at the end of it, not by the day-numbers.

## 6. FURTHER READING

**Ibn Ishaq, *Sirat Rasul Allah*, in Ibn Hisham's recension.** In English as A. Guillaume, *The Life of
Muhammad* (Oxford, 1955), still the standard translation; the Badr material runs from roughly p. 289 to p. 360.
The oldest continuous narrative of the battle and the spine of every account since, including this one. Read it
knowing what it is: a work of the 760s, surviving only in the edited version an Egyptian scholar made of it
sixty years later, by an author his own contemporaries in Medina accused of credulity about Jewish and
south-Arabian material and of taking reports from unreliable men. Guillaume prints Ibn Hisham's own notes
separately at the back, which is worth the flipping.

**al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*,** ed. Marsden Jones (3 vols, Oxford, 1966); English as Rizwi Faizer et al.,
*The Life of Muhammad: al-Waqidi's Kitab al-Maghazi* (Routledge, 2011). Fullest on the ground at Badr by a
distance: who stood where, what the wells were called, the state of the sand, the names of killers. It is
indispensable and it is the source you should trust least where it is most vivid, because the precision arrives
a generation after Ibn Ishaq's and nobody can say from where.

**Ibn Sa'd, *Kitab al-Tabaqat al-Kabir*.** The Badr expedition in vol. 2; the biographies of the participants in
vols 3 and 4, which is where the clan affiliations and the death-dates in the roster above mostly come from. Not
a narrative — a card index, and enormously useful as one, because it lets you check a man's clan without taking
a storyteller's word for it.

**al-Tabari, *Tarikh al-Rusul wa'l-Muluk*,** English as *The History of al-Tabari*, vol. 7, *The Foundation of
the Community*, trans. W. Montgomery Watt and M. V. McDonald (SUNY, 1987). Al-Tabari's method is to lay variant
reports side by side with their chains and let the reader judge, which makes him the single best place to see
what the disagreements over Badr actually look like before a modern author tidies them.

**Sahih al-Bukhari and Sahih Muslim,** *Kitab al-Maghazi* and *Kitab al-Jihad*. Short, disconnected reports, but
the most rigorously screened material we have, and on some points decisive: Ibn Mas'ud on finding Abu Jahl,
'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf on losing Umayya ibn Khalaf, the counting of the army. Useless for the shape of the day
and invaluable for particular moments in it.

**The Qur'an, Surat al-Anfal (8) entire, and Al 'Imran 3:121-129.** The earliest layer by two centuries and the
only one contemporary with the event, and the hardest to use, because it alludes rather than narrates: it
addresses people who were there and needed nothing explained. It nonetheless fixes several things the histories
only report — the dispute over the spoils, the reluctance of some to march, the rebuke over the ransoms.

**Ibn Kathir, *al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya*** and his ***al-Sira al-Nabawiyya*** (English trans. Trevor Le Gassick, 4
vols, Garnet, 1998-2000). A fourteenth-century synthesis that quotes its predecessors at length and argues
between them with a hadith critic's instincts. The most convenient single place to find the variant reports
gathered with an assessment attached, if you accept the assessor's premises.

**W. Montgomery Watt, *Muhammad at Medina*** (Oxford, 1956). The classic Western reconstruction, and the
sharpest thinking anyone has done about what the Badr expedition was for in terms of the caravan economy and the
politics of the oasis. Dated in idiom, occasionally too confident that a material motive must underlie a
religious one, and still the book to argue with.

**Martin Lings, *Muhammad: His Life Based on the Earliest Sources*** (Islamic Texts Society, 1983). A narrative
retelling, beautifully written, faithful to Ibn Ishaq and al-Waqidi and almost entirely uncritical of them: it
harmonises where the sources conflict rather than reporting the conflict. Read for the shape of the story and
not for adjudication.

**Fred M. Donner, *Muhammad and the Believers: At the Origins of Islam*** (Harvard, 2010), with his earlier
*Narratives of Islamic Origins* (Darwin, 1998). Donner takes the source-critical objections seriously without
concluding that nothing can be known, and *Narratives* is the best account of how the maghazi literature came to
be written and what that does to its reliability.

**Patricia Crone, *Meccan Trade and the Rise of Islam*** (Princeton, 1987). Sceptical, sometimes corrosive, and
essential background for anyone tempted to build an argument on the value of Abu Sufyan's caravan: Crone's
attack on the standard picture of Mecca as a great trading city is not wholly persuasive but it cannot be
ignored.

**Safi al-Rahman al-Mubarakpuri, *Ar-Raheeq Al-Makhtum* (The Sealed Nectar)** (Riyadh, various editions). A
modern devotional synthesis, widely read, with the clearest simple maps and marching-tables of the campaign in
print. Openly committed and not source-critical; useful for orientation and for the geography, and to be checked
against the classical texts on anything contested.
