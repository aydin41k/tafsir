# A Note on the Sources

No one who fought at Badr wrote an account of it. The battle was fought on a Friday morning in March 624 by some
thirteen hundred men in a dry valley three days' march from Medina, and the earliest continuous narrative of it
that survives was composed by a man who died roughly a hundred and forty years later. Between the event and the
book stand four or five generations of speech, and almost everything a reader should know about these sources
follows from that.

What we have comes in four layers of unequal weight. **The Qur'an** is the earliest by a long way, and the only
material contemporary with the men who were there. Surat al-Anfal — "the Spoils" — is a chapter of some seventy-five
verses which the tradition places at Badr and just after it. But it is not a chronicle. It never names Badr's day,
its commanders, its dead, or its numbers. It speaks of "the day of the two parties," of the lower ground and the
further ground, of a caravan the believers had wanted and a fight they had not; it rebukes them for quarrelling over
the spoils, calls the sleep that fell on them a security from God, and speaks of a thousand angels sent down. It is
addressed to men who needed no scene set, which makes it enormously valuable and almost useless for narrative — no
hour, no name, no line of speech.

**The maghazi and the sira** are where the story comes from. *Maghazi* means the raids: campaign literature. Its
foundation is Muhammad ibn Ishaq (d. 150/767), whose life of the Prophet survives not in his own words but in the
recension of Ibn Hisham (d. 218/833). Then al-Waqidi (d. 207/822), whose *Kitab al-Maghazi* treats the expeditions
alone and in far greater detail; Ibn Sa'd (d. 230/845), al-Waqidi's secretary, whose *Tabaqat* preserves the *badri*
lists, the roll of men who were at Badr and the most prized entry a Muslim family could claim; and al-Tabari (d.
310/923), precious for setting rival reports side by side instead of choosing between them. The distance is the
problem. Ibn Ishaq wrote in early Abbasid Iraq about a battle fought by the ancestors of men then disputing who
should rule, and tribes had claims to press: which clan's man struck down Abu Jahl stayed a question of family
honour for centuries, and the sources give three answers, each traceable to an interested house. The reigning
dynasty had an ancestor on the wrong side of the field, and reports about al-'Abbas soften with time.

**The hadith collections** were not written as history. Bukhari (d. 256/870) and Muslim (d. 261/875) pick up Badr
incidentally, wherever a legal point needed a precedent — in the books on campaigns, on the fifth share of plunder,
on funerals. But theirs is the most rigorously filtered material we have, because the hadith scholars developed the
*isnad*: the chain of transmitters, each named, running back to an eyewitness, with the reliability of every link
investigated and recorded. It did real work, and it has a limit its own builders stated. An isnad certifies that a
man of good memory said he heard this from another such man; it cannot certify that the first speaker remembered
accurately, and it constrains invention rather than the drift of a true story towards a better one.

**The poetry** was the Arab medium of record, composed at the time and held in place by its metre, which makes it
in principle the earliest narrative layer of all — and the least reliable in detail, because its business was to
magnify one clan and diminish another, and later partisans forged it freely. Verse is used here for atmosphere and
names, not for facts.

## The two who matter

Ibn Ishaq and al-Waqidi carry this chapter, and they fail in different directions.

Ibn Ishaq has shape. He tells Badr as a sequence with a logic to it: a caravan, a warning, two musters, a march, a
valley, wells, a night of rain, a morning. He names his informants often, and often they are descendants of
participants — the account of Abu Jahl's severed leg comes down in the family of the man who cut it off. He was
also criticised in his own lifetime. Malik ibn Anas, the great jurist of Medina, is reported to have called him a
liar; Ahmad ibn Hanbal thought him acceptable for campaign narrative but would not build law on him. The charges
were that he took material from Jewish and Christian informants, that he recited poetry of doubtful authenticity,
and — most substantially — that he was loose with his chains, writing "some of the people of knowledge told me"
where a hadith scholar wanted four names. The looseness is real: he is good on the frame and shakier inside it.
Ibn Hisham then states in his preface what he left out: matter with no bearing on the Prophet, poems no authority
recognised, things "which it would be offensive to discuss," and things that would distress people. That is a
candid editor with a policy, and we cannot see through him.

Al-Waqidi is the opposite case and the more seductive one. He knows the name of the hillock, the hour of the day,
the words a man said to his brother, the camels slaughtered at each halt, the coats of mail in each army; his
topography is precise enough that modern surveys have been able to test it against the terrain. That richness is
why he must be handled with tongs, because detail of such specificity does not survive two centuries of oral
transmission: it is either recovered from local knowledge or it is the filling-in a good storyteller does with
thin materials.

The tendency of the whole literature runs one way, towards harmony and towards wonder. Contradictions get reconciled
by later compilers, bare reports acquire circumstance, and divine help acquires numbers, then names, then faces. The
angels are the clearest case: the Qur'an says a thousand and describes no fighting; Ibn Ishaq adds a voice and a named
horse; al-Waqidi adds positions in the line; in the later popular literature they have biographies and wear yellow
turbans to match al-Zubayr's. That sequence tells us much about the material and little about Badr.

## How much of it is true

The sceptical position holds that the campaign narratives are largely secondary — that they grew out of the Qur'an
rather than alongside it. Later Muslims, on this reading, found allusive verses about a day of two parties, a caravan
and a thousand angels, and built the circumstances those allusions required, furnishing the result with names out of
tribal memory. In its strongest form it notes that the sira's Badr material maps onto Surat al-Anfal almost verse by
verse, which is what one would expect if the story had been made to explain the verses. It cannot be waved away.

The moderate position, where most working historians now sit — Montgomery Watt in *Muhammad at Medina* is its most
careful practitioner on Badr, and Fred Donner has argued the general case — concedes much of that about the texture
while insisting on a hard core. Names, places, outcomes and rough dates behave differently from dialogue and detail,
being the things a community organises itself around. The *badri* lists are the test case: to have been at Badr
conferred status, a stipend and precedence for generations, and a tradition inventing freely could not have held a
roll of three hundred names against three centuries of scrutiny by rival families.

Badr sits about as well in that framework as any event of its period can. That a battle was fought at its wells in
Ramadan of the second year after the hijra, following a failed interception of a Meccan caravan out of Syria; that
the Muslims were outnumbered and won; that 'Utba ibn Rabi'a, Abu Jahl and Umayya ibn Khalaf died there; that
prisoners were ransomed and the spoils caused a quarrel settled by revelation — these are as secure as anything known
about seventh-century Arabia. The hour-by-hour texture is not, and neither is the dialogue; and the chapter's most
vivid moments are dialogue.

## What this chapter did, and where to doubt it

Three rules govern what follows. The chapter commits in the prose and argues in the notes, so that a reader who
skips every note meets an unbroken story while one who reads them finds the disputes set out. It prefers the report
that costs somebody something — the version leaving a commander doubted, an army indisciplined, a Companion behaving
badly — because tidiness in this literature is usually a sign of handling. And it reports the religious material as
report: the dream of 'Atika bint 'Abd al-Muttalib, the sleep before the battle, the angels, the handful of dust are
given as the sources give them, with the attribution visible. To debunk them would substitute the historian's
metaphysics for the sources'; to assert them, the sources' for the reader's.

Four passages a careful reader should hold loosely. **The dream of 'Atika**, in which a rider cries in the valley of
Mecca that the treacherous people should go out to their deaths in three days, and the messenger from the caravan
arrives on the third: it comes through Hashimite channels, its ending fits its beginning too neatly, and its punchline
is al-'Abbas — ancestor of the Abbasid caliphs — scoring off Abu Jahl in public. **The speeches at Dhafiran**:
something was certainly said at that halt, but the clause about following the Prophet into the sea is attached to
three speakers in three sources, the signature of a formula rather than a memory. **Sawad ibn Ghaziyya and the
arrow-shaft**: the shaft used to straighten the line is secure, the bared belly is of a recognisable didactic type
and appears first in al-Waqidi. **The single combats**: champions certainly fought between the lines, but the
reported speech comes from a moment when few stood close enough to hear it.

To which add the whole middle of the battle. Thirteen hundred men and a hundred horses churning a dry valley floor
raise a dust-cloud inside which no one sees past the men alongside him, and any account that gives that melee a shape
is giving it one it did not have. The chapter tells the hour as a series of episodes because that is the form in which
the evidence reaches us, not because that is how it felt.
