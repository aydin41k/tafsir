# Appendix: What the Sources Disagree About

The chapter commits in its main text to one reading of each disputed point and marks the commitment
lightly, so that the narrative keeps moving. This appendix pays the debt. Four tests are applied
throughout: how early the report is, whether it is consistent with the rest of the account, whether
it is physically possible on the ground at Badr in March, and whose interests it serves. The last is
the sharpest and most easily abused, and is used only where the interest is plain.

---

**1. The size of the Qurayshi army.** Ibn Ishaq, through Ibn Hisham, gives "between nine hundred and
a thousand"; others round to a flat thousand; al-Waqidi allows a thousand or more; some later writers
put thirteen hundred out of Mecca, which need not contradict, since Banu Zuhra and Banu 'Adi turned
back at al-Juhfa and took perhaps three hundred men home. The most interesting figure is not a figure
but a calculation. Two captured water-carriers said nine or ten camels were slaughtered daily at the
halts, and the Prophet reckoned a hundred men to a camel. That the tradition preserves the method and
not only the answer counts in its favour; fabricators supply totals. Nine hundred and fifty is a good
ancient estimate, not a muster roll.

**2. The number of Qurayshi horses.** The maghazi writers agree on a hundred, with the richest men
mounted and barded. A minority report allows Quraysh only two — the same number the Muslims had —
which is almost certainly either a copyist's eye slipping between the two inventories or a homiletic
flattening meant to make the victory turn wholly on faith. Two things favour the hundred. No source
describes a Qurayshi cavalry charge, which is exactly what an inflator would have supplied: the horses
appear on the approach, in the flight, and nowhere between. And the ground explains their uselessness,
soft rain-wetted sand being no place for cavalry. A hundred idle horses is a stranger and
better-evidenced fact than two.

**3. The size and value of the caravan.** A thousand camels, goods worth some fifty thousand dinars,
thirty or forty men escorting. Some reports halve the camels to seven hundred; the escort is
occasionally raised to seventy. Fifty thousand arrives already rounded and means "the whole Meccan
trading capital of that season" — Ibn Hisham's own point when he says hardly a man or woman of Quraysh
with ten dinars had stayed out of it. The thousand camels are more defensible,
explaining the scale of the alarm. The small escort is the strongest detail of the three, the least
flattering to Quraysh and the one that makes the campaign intelligible.

**4. Whether the Muslims marched out for battle or for plunder.** The maghazi literature, written when
Badr had become the archetype of holy war, tends to present an army going out to meet an army. The
Qur'an, earlier by a century, says something harder: that God promised the believers one of
two parties, and they wanted the other one, the unarmed one (Q 8:5-8). The verse is unflattering,
which is why it must be early; no later apologist invents a rebuke that has the companions setting out
after baggage camels and vexed to find a war instead. The narrative sources corroborate it — three
hundred men with two horses and six coats of mail, and a consultation at Dhafiran necessary only
because the object had changed. The chapter follows the Qur'an.

**5. Who killed Abu Jahl.** Four names circulate, and the reports compete for credit rather than
contradict. Ibn Ishaq has Mu'adh ibn 'Amr ibn al-Jamuh break the ring of guards and take Abu Jahl's
leg off at the shin — Mu'adh's own image was of a date-stone spat from under a crusher — and then
Mu'awwidh ibn 'Afra beat the fallen man and pass on into the press, where he was killed. Bukhari
preserves a report in which Ibn Mas'ud finds Abu Jahl among the wounded, still breathing, sets a foot
on his neck and takes his head. Variants substitute Mu'adh ibn 'Afra for Mu'adh ibn 'Amr; later
accounts credit the whole to Ibn Mas'ud, or to 'Ali. The adopted sequence reconciles the substantial
reports and explains why each survived: three men could claim a hand in it.

**6. Who killed 'Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt, and where.** Not on the field. 'Uqba was taken alive and
executed on the march home, at 'Irq al-Zabya between al-Rawha' and Medina. Ibn Ishaq names 'Asim ibn
Thabit al-Ansari; a rival report gives the killing to 'Ali ibn Abi Talib, who had certainly carried
out the other roadside execution, that of al-Nadr ibn al-Harith at al-Safra'. The 'Ali version looks
like assimilation — two executions on one road within days, one securely attributed, the second drawn
after it. 'Asim is preferred and the killings kept distinct. Both traditions preserve 'Uqba's protest
that he alone of Quraysh had been singled out.

**7. Who killed Umayya ibn Khalaf, and Bilal's part.** The core is told by 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf
himself, and its survival in that form is the best argument for it, since it does its narrator no
credit. He had taken Umayya — a guest-friend from Mecca — and Umayya's son prisoner, and was leading
them to the rear when Bilal ibn Rabah, whom Umayya had owned and tortured, cried out that the head of
unbelief should not live while he lived. Ansar came at the shout and cut both men down in their
captor's hands, and 'Abd al-Rahman lost the ransom and said so. Variants make Bilal himself the killer,
which is tidier and therefore more suspect. A prisoner-taker robbed of his prize and grumbling about it
is not what traditions invent.

**8. Whether al-'Abbas fought willingly.** The Prophet's uncle was taken prisoner by Abu al-Yasar
Ka'b ibn 'Amr, a small man against a very large one, and a persistent tradition holds that he had come
out under compulsion, was secretly a Muslim, and had informed for the Muslims in Mecca. It matters
because the 'Abbasid caliphs, under whose patronage much of this literature was compiled, descended
from him. Against the exculpation stand details preserved even by writers sympathetic to him: that he
took his day in the rotation of camel-slaughtering that fed the marching army, which no man dragged
along would do; that the water-carriers named him among the great men of the column; and the Prophet's
answer to his plea, that outwardly he had been against them and must ransom himself and his kin. He
fought; the compulsion story is later and interested.

**9. The count of the dead and the prisoners.** Seventy killed and seventy taken is Ibn Ishaq's
figure and is repeated everywhere; the chapter uses it and admits at once that seventy is the kind of
number traditions like. The name-lists tell against exactness: Ibn Ishaq's own roll of the Qurayshi
dead runs to fewer than seventy, and al-Waqidi's differs from it both ways. The twenty-four chiefs
thrown into the well-pit are counted with more confidence, the great men being known by name where
their slaves were not. Where a formula and a name-list conflict, the list is earlier material. The
Muslim figure runs the other way — fourteen dead, six Muhajirun and eight Ansar, individually
commemorated; a rival count of twenty-two looks like the fourteen plus men who later died of wounds.

**10. The bearer of the second black banner.** Three standards are agreed: the white liwa' of the army
carried by Mus'ab ibn 'Umayr, whose clan held the hereditary right to it; the black rayah called
al-'Uqab, the Eagle, carried by 'Ali ibn Abi Talib; and a second black banner for the Ansar. The last
is disputed between Sa'd ibn Mu'adh, chief of the Aws, and al-Hubab ibn al-Mundhir of the Khazraj. The
day's logic favours al-Hubab: Sa'd spent the battle at the shelter as head of the Prophet's guard and
cannot have carried a banner in the line, and the Khazraj were much the larger of the two Medinan
confederations present. Some accounts give Aws and Khazraj a banner each, making four — unresolvable,
and the chapter follows al-Hubab without labouring it.

**11. The date and the day.** The great majority of reports give 17 Ramadan of the year 2; a minority
prefers 19 or 21, and the variance is explained by disagreement over whether the month began on the
crescent seen at Mecca or at Medina — a day either way, propagating through every later reckoning. The
conversion to 13 March 624 rests on retrojecting the lunar calendar from the era of the Hijra; it is
arithmetic, not observation, and is sound to within a day or two and no better. The tradition that it
was a Friday is early, bound up with the naming of the day as the yawm al-furqan, and the calculation
supports it. Read "about the middle of March" behind the date.

**12. The angels: a thousand, three thousand, or five thousand.** Three Qur'anic passages give three
figures, and the older commentators were alive to the problem. Surat al-Anfal has God answer the
believers' cry with a thousand angels, rank on rank (Q 8:9). Al 'Imran, which most commentators also
referred to Badr, has three thousand sent down (Q 3:124) and then, conditionally, five thousand if
they held steady (Q 3:125). The classical resolution is sequential: a thousand promised, three
thousand sent, five thousand contingent on conduct. An alternative reading, also old, refers the
Al 'Imran verses to Uhud, where the condition was conspicuously not met. What is not in doubt is the
direction of growth. Ibn Ishaq and al-Waqidi supply named angels, Jibril in a yellow turban, men
hearing a whip crack overhead; the Qur'anic layer is allusive and names nobody.

**13. Whether the duels happened as described.** The single combats — mubaraza — are among the
best-attested episodes of the day and the most formally shaped. Ibn Ishaq has three young Ansar step
out first, 'Awf and Mu'awwidh the sons of 'Afra with 'Abdallah ibn Rawaha, and be sent back;
the Prophet then called for men of Quraysh's own kin, and 'Ubayda ibn al-Harith, Hamza and 'Ali went
out against 'Utba ibn Rabi'a, Shayba ibn Rabi'a and al-Walid ibn 'Utba. Some accounts omit the Ansar
altogether. Their rejection is preferred because it is unflattering to the Ansar, whose descendants
were telling this story in Medina a century later, and because it supplies a motive the shorter
versions lose: the challengers had demanded equals of their own blood. The shape is suspiciously neat;
the substance stands.

**14. Abu al-Bakhtari and the exemption order.** Certain men of Quraysh were named as not to be
killed if they could be taken — men who had defended the Prophet in Mecca or refused to join the
boycott of Banu Hashim — and Abu al-Bakhtari ibn Hisham was among them. Ibn Ishaq has al-Mujadhdhir
ibn Dhiyad al-Balawi find him, tell him he was exempt, and be asked about his companion, for whom
al-Mujadhdhir had no order. Abu al-Bakhtari would not be spared alone: the women of Mecca should never
say he had abandoned his friend to save himself. He was killed. The lists generally include him,
though at least one confuses him with his kinsman al-'As ibn Hisham. The exemption order is sometimes
doubted as retrospective softening, but nobody gained by inventing an order a Qurayshi refused.

**15. The ransoms and the literate prisoners.** The reported range runs from a thousand dirhams for a
poor man to four thousand for a chief, with some sources naming four hundred for the poorest. Scaling
ransom to standing is plausible in itself — blood-money worked the same way — but the round thousands
are bands, not receipts. Two exceptions are well attested: Abu 'Azza al-Jumahi, a penniless poet with
five daughters, released on a promise never to lampoon the Muslims again; and al-Muttalib ibn Hantab,
let go because nobody of his people was there to pay. The report that literate captives
ransomed themselves by teaching Medinan children to write — ten children each, in the commonest
version — has no isnad strong enough to settle it, and ten is the sort of number that gets supplied.
Something of the kind is credible in a town where writing was scarce.

**16. Why Quraysh kept marching after the caravan was safe.** Ibn Ishaq's account turns on the
caravan: the alarm, the muster, Abu Sufyan's word that the danger was past, and a column that had
begun to turn round when Abu Jahl shamed it forward. Al-Waqidi's fuller council makes the second
motive explicit, that the road itself was at stake and a Medina able to threaten the Syrian traffic
would have to be broken. These are not alternatives, and the chapter takes them in sequence. What
settles the shape is 'Amir ibn al-Hadrami rising with his shirt torn to demand vengeance for his
brother, killed at Nakhla: a blood-claim publicly raised could not be set aside without dishonour.
That is how a rescue no longer needed became a war, and it is too awkward to be invention.

**17. The rain and the sleep.** Surat al-Anfal speaks of a slumber sent as security and of water sent
down to firm the believers' feet (Q 8:11). The narrative sources supply the squall: rain in the small
hours, firming the gravel on the Muslim bank and turning the soft sand where Quraysh were descending
al-'Aqanqal into heavy going. Whether the verse describes the weather or the weather was inferred from
the verse is answered by the ground: the contrast between the banks is a real feature of the place, and
the accounts of the Qurayshi descent — slow, loud, disordered, the horses labouring — read as
observation rather than exegesis.

**18. Hubab, the wells, and whether they were stopped.** Ibn Ishaq has al-Hubab ibn al-Mundhir ask
whether the halting-place was revelation or judgement, and on being told judgement, advise moving up
to the nearest well on the Qurayshi side, taking it, and building a cistern. Some read the episode as
a doublet, the same decision told once as the Prophet's and once as a companion's. The substance is
unimpeachable: the Muslims ended the night holding the water and Quraysh did not. Whether the further
wells were filled in or merely denied is not recoverable, and matters little — an army that cannot
reach a well is as thirsty as one whose well is choked.

---

The pattern of these disagreements is more instructive than any one of them. The tradition is firm
about ground, order and sequence: where the wells were, which bank each army held, that the rain fell,
that the duels came before the general engagement, that fourteen Muslims died and their names were
these. It drifts in three places only. Round numbers grow and tidy themselves. Credit multiplies, as
the descendants of the men involved acquire reasons to care. And the supernatural material, spare and
allusive in the Qur'an, becomes elaborate and named in writers working a century later. The drift is
not random, and it is not evidence that the whole is fiction: it is what happens to remembered fact
passing through generations of men with claims to press — tribal, dynastic, doctrinal — who could
press them only in the gaps of a story already too well known to alter. Abu Jahl acquires killers;
nobody acquires a victory Quraysh did not lose. The most useful habit here is to ask of any detail
whether it flatters its transmitter. Much of what survives from Badr does not, and that is the
strongest argument these sources make on their own behalf.
