## 2. The opening

Not "In the year 624." The chapter opens on one man's hands. Abu Sufyan ibn Harb, coming north up
the coast road with the caravan, stops at the water of Badr and reads the ground — the scene Ibn
Ishaq gives him, in which he crumbles camel dung and finds date-stones in it, deduces that riders
from Muhammad's town have watered there a night ahead of him, and turns the caravan for the sea.
It is the whole chapter in miniature: a small act of attention, a swerve, and a battle that follows
from the swerve rather than from anyone's intention.

**The actual first three sentences. Drafter of s1 must open with these, or with something that
matches this pitch exactly:**

> Abu Sufyan ibn Harb crouched at the water of Badr and broke a lump of camel dung apart in his
> fingers. There were date-stones in it. He stood up and said, to nobody in particular, that this
> was the fodder of Yathrib.

Then one short paragraph establishing that the two riders who had watered there a night ahead of
him had come from Muhammad's town and had come looking for him — and then the chapter steps back
to explain why.

## 3. The argument (through-line)

**PRIMARY: Badr was a collision that neither side set out to fight.**

Every stage of it was contingent, and the sources — which had every theological reason to present
the day as foreordained — preserve the contingency so plainly that it must be historical. A raiding
party of three hundred went out for a caravan and not for an army; the Qur'an itself tells the
believers they had wanted "the other of the two parties," the unarmed one. Abu Sufyan got the
caravan away and sent word to Mecca that the danger was past; the Meccan column had actually begun
to turn round when it was shamed forward. Two clans went home. The armies did not find each other
by design but because there was only one water in that stretch of the Hijaz in March, and both of
them needed it. Two water-parties met at a well in the dark. Then, and only then, was there no way
out.

This governs emphasis. It means: generous space for the caravan hunt, Abu Sufyan's swerve, the
Meccan false start and 'Utba ibn Rabi'a's attempt at peace; the water and the ground treated as
causes rather than as scenery; and an ending that measures what an unintended battle destroyed.

**Two subordinate threads, present throughout but never allowed to take the wheel:**

- *Ground and water as mechanism.* The wadi, the dunes of al-'Aqanqal, the wells, the rain, the
  hard sand on the Muslim side and the soft on the Meccan. These explain HOW the collision became
  a defeat, once it had become a battle.
- *Kinship as the wound.* The sources cannot stop noticing that this was a battle of close
  relatives, and the chapter should not stop noticing either — but as recurring detail (a son
  asking permission to kill his father, a man calling his uncle out of the line, brothers on
  opposite sides), never as a thesis paragraph. Let it accumulate and pay off in s6 and s7.

**Explicitly NOT the primary argument, though both get their say in s7:** that Badr turned on faith
rather than ground; that Badr was primarily the destruction of Meccan clan seniority. The second of
these is the *consequence* the chapter argues for in s7, not its through-line.

## 4. Apparatus policy

### 4.1 Length

- Chapter body: **11,000–13,000 words**, target 12,000.
- **80 endnotes** (hard range 55–85). Per-section allocations below; a drafter may go two over or
  under their allocation, no more.
- Back matter (not counted in the 12,000): "A Note on the Sources" (~900 words, the one place where
  the weighing of sources is done at length), "A Note on Numbers and Distances" (~350), "The
  Fallen" (the two casualty lists, ~250), "Glossary" (~250).

### 4.2 Citation format in the notes

One house form, no bibliography-style clutter:

- `Ibn Ishaq, in Guillaume, *The Life of Muhammad*, 296.` — Guillaume's pagination where the
  drafter is genuinely confident of it; otherwise `Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of
  the raid on the caravan of Quraysh.`
- `al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr.` — cite by section, never by invented page.
- `Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids).` / `al-Tabari, *Tarikh*, the year 2 AH.` /
  `Ibn Kathir, *al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya*, iii.`
- `Bukhari, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Maghazi, bab of the Badr expedition.` / `Muslim, *Sahih*, Kitab
  al-Jihad wa'l-Siyar.` Hadith by book-and-chapter, never by number unless certain.
- Qur'an as `Q 8:5–7`, in prose as "Surat al-Anfal" with the verse in the note.
- Moderns in full on first appearance in the notes, short thereafter: `W. Montgomery Watt,
  *Muhammad at Medina*, ch. 1.` `Fred M. Donner, "Mecca's Food Supplies and Muhammad's Boycott."`
  `Martin Lings, *Muhammad: His Life Based on the Earliest Sources*, ch. 40.`
  `Safi-ur-Rahman al-Mubarakpuri, *Ar-Raheeq Al-Makhtum*, "The Battle of Badr."`
- **Never fabricate precision.** If the page is not known, the section is cited. A drafter who
  invents a page number has broken the chapter.

### 4.3 Sources in the main text versus the notes

- **Main text:** names authorities *bare and singular* — "Ibn Ishaq has him say", "al-Waqidi, who
  is fullest on the ground at Badr, puts", "Bukhari preserves a report from Ibn Mas'ud". Never a
  work's title in the body except *Sirat Rasul Allah* and *Kitab al-Maghazi*, once each, at their
  first appearance. Never more than **one** authority named per sentence and rarely more than one
  per contested moment; the rival is pushed to the note.
- **Notes:** carry the full citation, the rival reports, the isnad point if it matters, and the
  argument. Notes may be mini-essays of 150–250 words where the dispute deserves it; most should be
  one or two sentences.
- **Modern scholars appear in the notes only**, with one exception: s7 may name Watt once in the
  body.

### 4.4 How a contested point is signalled in prose without stalling

Commit, mark lightly, move. The permitted signal phrases (use them, vary them, do not exceed one
clause):

- "Ibn Ishaq's figure, which I follow, is …"
- "most probably", "on the better reports", "the earliest layer has it that"
- "al-Waqidi alone says …"
- "The tradition is divided about who struck first; the likeliest sequence is …"
- "Later writers were uncomfortable with this, and it shows."

**Banned in the body:** "scholars disagree"; "it is impossible to know"; any "some say … while
others say … and a third group says" chain; any sentence whose subject is the historiography rather
than the event; parenthetical source-hedges longer than four words. A reader skipping every note
must never meet a sentence that does not move.

### s1 — The Fodder of Yathrib  (1,500 words, 9 notes)

**Narrative job.** Open on Abu Sufyan's hands at the water; then reach back and lay the causes of
the collision in the plainest possible terms — the Emigration, the confiscated property, the
coastal road, the sequence of failed intercepts through 1–2 AH — and bring the reader forward to the
moment Talha ibn 'Ubaydallah and Sa'id ibn Zayd come back to Medina with news that the caravan is
coming down from Gaza. Establish the register: concrete, unhurried, sourced.

**Events.** Abu Sufyan at Badr reading the dung. What Yathrib/Medina was and who lived in it; the
Muhajirun (the emigrants from Mecca, who arrived with nothing) and the Ansar (the "helpers", the men
of Aws and Khazraj who took them in) explained inline in one sentence each. The property left behind
in Mecca and what it meant to see it moving up the road as somebody else's cargo. The coastal route
from Mecca to Syria and why it mattered — that Mecca lived on it and Medina sat beside it. The
earlier expeditions that found nothing: Waddan, Buwat, al-'Ushayra, Nakhla, and the affair at Nakhla
in a sacred month that had already made bad blood. The caravan's departure from Gaza; Talha and
Sa'id ibn Zayd watching the road at al-Hawra'. The muster order in Medina.

**People who must appear.** Abu Sufyan ibn Harb; the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ (first mention with glyph);
Talha ibn 'Ubaydallah; Sa'id ibn Zayd; 'Abdallah ibn Jahsh (Nakhla) in a clause; Damdam ibn 'Amr
al-Ghifari named only at the *end of s1 or start of s2* — assign to s2, see below.

**Detail-hooks (use at least five).** Date-stones in the dung. The weight and price of a camel-load
of Syrian goods. That the coast road ran within about eighty miles of Medina at its nearest — near
enough for a raiding party on camels to reach it and be back in a fortnight. Ramadan: the month, the
fast, and that the Muslims fought this battle in it. Two horses in the whole army. The
distance-to-time conversion a reader needs: a loaded caravan making perhaps twenty-five miles a day.
The Medina heat and the state of the wells in March.

**Contested points owned.** #11 caravan size (note). #2 the object of the expedition — *begin* the
argument here; do not finish it, s2 finishes it. Also in note: whether the earlier expeditions were
raids or reconnaissance, and Donner's argument about Mecca's food supply and a Medinan squeeze on
the road (note only).

**First line.** `Abu Sufyan ibn Harb crouched at the water of Badr and broke a lump of camel dung apart in his fingers.`

**Last line.** `The order went round Medina that afternoon, and it was not an order to prepare for a battle.`

**MUST NOT COVER.** Damdam's ride and the Meccan muster (s2). The Dhafiran council (s2). Any
description of the wadi's ground (s3). Any casualty or prisoner material. Any theology of Q 8.

### s2 — Three Hundred and Thirteen  (1,700 words, 12 notes)

**Narrative job.** Two musters, in parallel, both of them improvised. Establish the asymmetry of
equipment and the peculiar political fact that the Prophet asked the Ansar for their consent to
something outside the letter of their agreement — and got it. End with both columns on the road and
neither of them yet aimed at the other.

**Events.** Damdam ibn 'Amr al-Ghifari's ride to Mecca with Abu Sufyan's appeal; the slit ears of
his camel and the ritual of the alarm. 'Atika bint 'Abd al-Muttalib's dream, three nights before
Damdam came — narrated as Ibn Ishaq narrates it, attributed, with al-'Abbas carrying it to Abu
Jahl's mockery. The Meccan muster, clan by clan; Abu Lahab staying behind and hiring al-'As ibn
Hisham in his place; Umayya ibn Khalaf shamed into going by 'Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt with a censer and
kohl-stick. The Muslim march out with 313: the count, the two horses, the seventy camels ridden in
turns, the Prophet sharing a camel with 'Ali and Marthad. The camp at Dhafiran and the news that
the Meccan army was out; the council; Abu Bakr and 'Umar speaking; al-Miqdad; and then Sa'd ibn
Mu'adh's speech for the Ansar — the crucial constitutional moment, because the Ansar's pact bound
them to defend Medina and not to march beyond it.

**People.** Damdam ibn 'Amr al-Ghifari; 'Atika bint 'Abd al-Muttalib; al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib;
Abu Jahl ('Amr ibn Hisham); Abu Lahab; Umayya ibn Khalaf; 'Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt; Abu Bakr; 'Umar ibn
al-Khattab; al-Miqdad ibn 'Amr; Sa'd ibn Mu'adh; 'Ali ibn Abi Talib; Marthad ibn Abi Marthad;
Mus'ab ibn 'Umayr (the white banner); Ibn Umm Maktum or Abu Lubaba left in charge of Medina.

**Detail-hooks.** Sixty coats of mail on the Meccan side against six; eight swords among 313. Seventy
camels for 313 men and what that means — three men to a camel, walking in rotation, in a fasting
month. The Prophet's own place in the rotation. That two clans, Banu 'Adi and (in some reports) Banu
Zuhra's neighbours, hesitated from the first. The slit ears of the alarm-camel. The censer and
kohl-stick as an accusation of womanishness. The count 313 and that the tradition remembers it as
the number of Saul's men — put the parallel in a note, not the body.

**Contested points owned.** #9 Muslim numbers and equipment (note, the fullest note in this section).
#12 the Ansari speaker (note). #2 the object of the expedition — *finish* it here: Q 8:5–7 and "the
other of the two parties", handled in the body in one tight passage. Also in notes: whether the
Dhafiran council is one occasion or a composite of several; whether 'Atika's dream is a genuinely
early report or a piece of Hashimite family tradition.

**First line.** `Damdam ibn 'Amr al-Ghifari came into the valley of Mecca on a camel whose ears he had slit and whose nose he had bloodied, standing in his saddle, his shirt turned back to front.`

**Last line.** `Two columns were now on the road, and each of them was still looking for something other than the other.`

**MUST NOT COVER.** Abu Sufyan's swerve at Badr (s1 has the scene; s3 has the consequence). The
Meccan turn-back and Zuhra's departure (s3). The ground of the wadi (s3). Any fighting.

### s3 — The Valley of Yalyal  (1,900 words, 14 notes)

**Narrative job.** The geography section, and the section where the collision becomes unavoidable.
This is where the ground is taught to the reader as a cause. It contains the chapter's two great
"almosts": Abu Sufyan's message that the caravan is safe and the Meccan column's half-turn, and
'Utba ibn Rabi'a's proposal to pay the blood-money for Ibn al-Hadrami and go home. It ends with both
armies committed and the Muslims holding the water.

**Events.** The wadi described — what a wadi is, in one clause, without lecturing. The plain of Badr:
the seasonal watercourse of Yalyal running north-west to the sea; the wells on the Medinan (eastern)
side; the great sandhill of al-'Aqanqal on the Meccan (western) side and the soft red sand beneath
it; the hard-packed ground on the east; the two hills, and the pass Quraysh had to come through. The
Muslim scouts — 'Ali, al-Zubayr, Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas — taking two Qurayshi water-boys at the wells
and what the boys said about the numbers (the loaves: nine or ten camels slaughtered a day, hence the
count). Abu Sufyan's swerve and his message; Quraysh beginning to turn; Abu Jahl's refusal; the
killing of 'Amr ibn al-Hadrami at Nakhla brought forward as the debt that keeps them there.
Banu Zuhra under al-Akhnas ibn Shariq going home, and Banu 'Adi never coming. 'Utba's speech and Abu
Jahl's answer to it; 'Amir ibn al-Hadrami's counter-appeal. The Muslim advance to the wells; Hubab
ibn al-Mundhir's question — "is this a place God has ordered you to, or is it opinion and stratagem?"
— and the move forward to the nearest water. The wells behind the Muslim line.

**People.** 'Utba ibn Rabi'a; Shayba ibn Rabi'a; al-Walid ibn 'Utba; Abu Jahl; 'Amir ibn al-Hadrami;
al-Akhnas ibn Shariq; Hakim ibn Hizam (the messenger between 'Utba and Abu Jahl); Hubab ibn
al-Mundhir; 'Ali ibn Abi Talib; al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam; Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas; Basbas ibn 'Amr and
'Adi ibn Abi'l-Zaghba' (the two scouts of s1 — name them here for the payoff); the two water-boys,
one of them Aslam.

**Detail-hooks.** The loaves-and-camels arithmetic as the period's way of counting an army. Nine or
ten camels a day for the Meccan host. One hundred horses on one side and two on the other. Seven
hundred Meccan camels. That the Meccan army had brought singing-women and a wine-skin economy with
it, and that 'Utba was over sixty. The distance from the Meccan camp to the wells. That the water at
Badr in mid-March would have been the only reliable supply for a day's march in any direction. The
grade of al-'Aqanqal and what descending soft sand does to a horse.

**Contested points owned.** #1 Qurayshi numbers (PROSE, with the fullest note in the chapter: Ibn
Ishaq's c. 950, al-Waqidi's tallies, the 1,000 of the round tradition, the minimalist case, and why
c. 900–1,000 is defensible from the loaves report). #3 why Quraysh marched (PROSE). #10 horses
(note). #13 Hubab and the wells (note). Also in notes: whether al-Akhnas took 300 men or 100 away;
whether 'Utba's peace proposal is a genuine report or a Rabi'a-family apologia — treat seriously,
prefer genuine, and say why (it makes 'Utba look weak, which is not how family tradition works).

**First line.** `The plain of Badr lay about eighty miles south-west of Medina, where the caravan road from Syria bent inland from the sea.`

**Last line.** `By the time the sun went down on the sixteenth of Ramadan, the Muslims had the water behind them and Quraysh had a sandhill at their backs.`

**MUST NOT COVER.** The rain and the night (s4). The battle order or the mubaraza (s4/s5). The
prisoners (s6). Any casualty figures.

### s4 — The Night of Rain  (1,700 words, 11 notes)

**Narrative job.** The last night and the first hour: preparation, prayer, the drawing up of lines,
the challenge to single combat, the opening of the fight. This section holds the chapter's religious
material at its densest, and it must be the most disciplined about register. Ends at the moment the
lines close.

**Events.** The rain in the night — heavy on the Meccan side, softening the sand; light on the
Muslim side, packing it. The sleep that Ibn Ishaq and the Qur'an both describe falling on the Muslim
camp. The Prophet praying through the night by a tree. The 'arish — the shelter of palm-branches
built for him on the rise, with Abu Bakr, and Sa'd ibn Mu'adh posting a guard of Ansar; and Sa'd's
proposal to have camels ready in case the day went badly, which the Prophet's own reserve about the
outcome makes credible. The dawn, the date, the fast (and that the sources differ on whether the
army fasted that day). The Prophet drawing up the line himself with an arrow-shaft in his hand,
straightening it; the prod that bruised Sawad ibn Ghaziyya's belly and Sawad's demand for redress.
The Meccan advance through the pass and down the dune. The first blood: al-Aswad ibn 'Abd al-Asad
al-Makhzumi going for the water-cistern and killed by Hamza. The mubaraza — the formal single combats
with which an Arab battle opened, explained in a clause: three Ansar offered first and refused by
'Utba, who asked for men of his own kin; then 'Ubayda ibn al-Harith, Hamza and 'Ali against 'Utba,
Shayba and al-Walid. The result. The Prophet's supplication in the shelter, and Abu Bakr's answer to
it — narrated as the tradition narrates it. The lines closing.

**People.** The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ; Abu Bakr; Sa'd ibn Mu'adh; Sawad ibn Ghaziyya; al-Aswad ibn
'Abd al-Asad; Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib; 'Ali ibn Abi Talib; 'Ubayda ibn al-Harith; 'Utba, Shayba,
al-Walid; 'Awf and Mu'awwidh, the sons of 'Afra, and 'Abdallah ibn Rawaha (the three Ansar refused);
Bilal, briefly, if it can be done without crowding.

**Detail-hooks.** Rain on sand and what it does to footing — one sentence, no more, it has already
been set up in s3. The palm-branch shelter. The arrow-shaft used as a measuring rod. Sawad's belly
and the Prophet lifting his shirt to let him strike back. 'Ubayda's leg and that he died of the
wound on the way home. The cloak slipping from the Prophet's shoulders in the shelter. The three
hundred men standing in a fasting month at first light with eight swords among them — reprise the
figure once, do not re-argue it.

**Contested points owned.** #14 the rain and Q 8:11 (note). #17 the date (note). Also in notes:
whether the army broke the fast that day; whether the mubaraza involved the three Ansar first (Ibn
Ishaq) or went straight to the Hashimites; the several versions of the Prophet's supplication and
which are early; whether Sa'd's camel proposal is authentic or a later softening.

**Register warning for this drafter.** Every supernatural or providential element in this section is
reported, attributed, and left standing: "Ibn Ishaq reports", "the Qur'an tells the believers",
"Bukhari preserves". Never asserted in the narrator's voice. Never explained away. Never sneered at.
If a drafter cannot tell the difference, they should not write s4.

**First line.** `It rained in the night.`

**Last line.** `Then the lines closed, and after that nobody could see the whole of it.`

**MUST NOT COVER.** The killing of Abu Jahl (s5). The rout, the angels' intervention, the handful of
dust (s5). Prisoners (s6).

### s5 — Above the Necks  (1,900 words, 12 notes)

**Narrative job.** The battle itself, from the closing of the lines to the collapse of the Meccan
centre and the death of Abu Jahl. The hardest section to write, because a melee has no shape: solve
it by moving through a sequence of named, locatable episodes rather than attempting a continuous
tactical account, and say so once, plainly, in a single sentence that does not stall.

**Events.** The Muslim line holding and being told to hold — the order not to break until given the
word, and the archery. The turn from volley to hand-to-hand. The handful of dust and "shahat
al-wujuh" (let the faces be disfigured), reported and attributed. The angels: Q 8:9's thousand and
the reports of what men said they saw and heard; handled in the tradition's own register, with the
Ibn 'Abbas report of the horseman heard above the dust. 'Ukkasha ibn Mihsan's sword breaking and the
palm-branch he was given in its place. Abu Jahl's stand in the Makhzumi centre. The two boys of the
Ansar, Mu'adh ibn 'Amr ibn al-Jamuh and Mu'awwidh ibn 'Afra, coming at him from either side; Mu'adh's
stroke to Abu Jahl's leg, the answering blow from 'Ikrima that left Mu'adh's arm hanging by skin
until he tore it off and fought on. Mu'awwidh killed later in the fight. 'Abdallah ibn Mas'ud finding
Abu Jahl among the wounded and the exchange between them. Umayya ibn Khalaf and 'Abd al-Rahman ibn
'Awf — the old Meccan friend who tried to take him prisoner and could not save him, one of the
chapter's kinship-and-obligation set pieces. Hamza, 'Ali, al-Zubayr's yellow turban, Abu Dujana.
Abu 'Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah and his father al-Jarrah; Abu Bakr's son 'Abd al-Rahman; the Meccan flight
up the sand.

**People.** Abu Jahl; Mu'adh ibn 'Amr ibn al-Jamuh; Mu'awwidh ibn 'Afra; 'Abdallah ibn Mas'ud;
'Ikrima ibn Abi Jahl; Umayya ibn Khalaf; 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf; Bilal; Hamza; 'Ali; al-Zubayr;
'Ukkasha ibn Mihsan; Abu Dujana; Abu 'Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah; Hanzala ibn Abi Sufyan (killed);
Mu'awwidh's brother 'Awf.

**Detail-hooks.** Eight swords, six mail-coats — the fighting was done mostly with spears, bows,
stones and bare hands, and the chapter should say so once here. The palm-branch that became a sword
in 'Ukkasha's hand and was kept in his family for generations. Mu'adh's arm. Ibn Mas'ud standing on
Abu Jahl's neck; Abu Jahl asking who had the upper hand and then asking to be struck high, at the
neck, so the head would look well when it was carried. Bilal's shout at Umayya. Al-Zubayr's yellow
turban as the angels' sign in the later reports (note). The dust, and that dust is the reason no
account of the middle of the battle can be trusted in detail.

**Contested points owned.** #5 who killed Abu Jahl (PROSE, with the sequence committed to and a
substantial note on Bukhari's version, Ibn Ishaq's, al-Waqidi's, and the two Ansari claims). #7 the
angels (PROSE in reportive register; long note on the 1,000/3,000/5,000 problem and the different
occasions of the verses). #8 the handful of dust (note). Also in notes: whether the Prophet fought
in person; whether the order to shoot and not to charge is a genuine tactical memory; the growth of
the named-angel material in the later sources.

**First line.** `The Muslims did not move.`

**Last line.** `The head was carried back across the wadi and laid at the Prophet's feet, and he said that it was the Pharaoh of this community.`

**MUST NOT COVER.** The prisoners, the pit, the ransom debate (s6). Casualty totals (s6). The march
home (s6/s7).

### s6 — The Pit and the Prisoners  (1,900 words, 13 notes)

**Narrative job.** Consequence at close range. The dead counted and disposed of; the prisoners taken,
argued over, ransomed or killed; the family scenes; the news arriving in both cities. The kinship
thread pays off here. Ends with Mecca in mourning and Medina holding seventy men it did not know what
to do with.

**Events.** The end of the fighting and the count: about seventy Meccan dead and about seventy taken;
fourteen Muslims dead, six Muhajirun and eight Ansar, named in the back matter and not in the body.
The pit at Badr and the bodies put into it; the Prophet's words over it and 'A'isha's later objection
about whether the dead can hear (note). 'Utba's body and Abu Hudhayfa ibn 'Utba's face when he saw
it. The two executions on the road home: al-Nadr ibn al-Harith at al-Safra' and 'Uqba ibn Abi
Mu'ayt at 'Irq al-Zabya, with what 'Uqba said and what he was answered. The dispute over the spoils
and Q 8:1, and the rule that came out of it. The debate over the prisoners: 'Umar for killing them,
Abu Bakr for ransom, the ransom taken, and Surat al-Anfal 67–69 rebuking it — handled in the body,
with the note admitting that the tidy Abu-Bakr-versus-'Umar shape of the story is probably a later
tidying of a messier argument. The prisoners who taught children to read in lieu of ransom. Al-'Abbas
and his ransom and the question of whether he was there willingly. Abu'l-'As ibn al-Rabi' and Zaynab's
necklace. Suhayl ibn 'Amr and 'Umar's request to pull his teeth. The news reaching Medina by
'Abdallah ibn Rawaha and Zayd ibn Haritha; the news reaching Mecca by al-Haysuman ibn 'Abdallah, and
Abu Lahab's interrogation of Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith, and Umm al-Fadl's blow with the tent-pole; the
Meccan ban on public mourning; al-Aswad ibn al-Muttalib's blindness and his sons.

**People.** Abu Hudhayfa ibn 'Utba; al-Nadr ibn al-Harith; 'Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt; 'Asim ibn Thabit;
'Ali; 'Umar; Abu Bakr; al-'Abbas; Abu'l-'As ibn al-Rabi'; Zaynab bint Muhammad; Suhayl ibn 'Amr;
'Abdallah ibn Rawaha; Zayd ibn Haritha; al-Haysuman ibn 'Abdallah al-Khuza'i; Abu Sufyan ibn
al-Harith; Abu Lahab; Umm al-Fadl (Lubaba); Sawda/Umm Hakim as available; Khubayb-family material
excluded (later).

**Detail-hooks.** The pit — a disused well, not a grave dug for the purpose. Four thousand dirhams
as a common ransom and one hundred for a poor man; the literate prisoners set to teaching ten
children each. The necklace Zaynab sent to buy her husband and what the Prophet said about it. Abu
Lahab dead of a sore within a week of the news. The tent-pole. That no woman in Mecca was permitted
to weep aloud for fear of giving Muhammad satisfaction.

**Contested points owned.** #4 al-'Abbas (PROSE, with the long note: the compulsion story, the secret
Islam story, and the 'Abbasid interest in both). #6 'Uqba's executioner (PROSE, half a clause; note
for the 'Ali variant). #15 casualties (PROSE, brief; note carries the arithmetic and the doubt about
seventy). #16 the ransom debate (PROSE; note on the sequence and on 'Umar's report in Muslim). #18
the pit and 'A'isha's objection (note).

**First line.** `The pit was an old well on the floor of the wadi that had fallen in and gone dry.`

**Last line.** `In Mecca no woman was allowed to weep where she could be heard.`

**MUST NOT COVER.** The fighting itself (s5). Analysis of what the battle meant (s7). Uhud, the
Trench, or anything after 3 AH (s7 only, and briefly).

### s7 — What Badr Broke  (1,400 words, 9 notes)

**Narrative job.** The reckoning. Close the through-line: an unintended battle with permanent
consequences. Argue, in the chapter's own voice and briefly, that the specific thing Badr destroyed
was the generation of men in whom Meccan authority resided — not Mecca's wealth, not its army, but
its seniority — and that this is why a skirmish of a thousand men against three hundred mattered out
of all proportion to its size. Then set down honestly what can and cannot be known.

**Events and material.** The return to Medina and the reception. The dead of Quraysh read as a list
of offices rather than of men: 'Utba and Shayba of 'Abd Shams, Abu Jahl and his brother's line in
Makhzum, Umayya ibn Khalaf of Jumah, Zam'a, al-Harith, al-Nadr — the men who sat in the council, the
guarantors of the pacts, the holders of the caravan shares. That Abu Sufyan, who had been outside the
first rank, inherited the leadership by survival; that Mecca's response was necessarily a war of
revenge rather than of policy; and that Uhud a year later follows from the pit at Badr. Inside Medina:
the change in the standing of the Muhajirun; the Ansar's investment now irreversible; the position
of the Jewish tribes and of the uncommitted, with the Banu Qaynuqa' named and their expulsion placed
correctly in time but not narrated. The Qur'anic settlement — Surat al-Anfal as a document of the
battle's aftermath rather than its course, the fifth, the prisoners, the naming of the day as *yawm
al-furqan*, the day of distinction. The status of *badri* — that a man who had been at Badr carried it
for the rest of his life, that the lists were kept, and that this is both why we know so many names
and why we should be careful of the lists. One paragraph, no more, on what a source-critical reader
should hold back on: the numbers, the speeches, the order of the fighting. And a last look at the
contingency: the caravan got through, and Abu Sufyan sold it in Mecca.

**People.** Abu Sufyan ibn Harb (closing the ring opened in the chapter's first sentence); the dead
of s6 recalled as offices; Watt named once in the body; Banu Qaynuqa' as a collective.

**Detail-hooks.** The caravan's cargo reaching Mecca safely and being sold — the object of the whole
expedition, obtained by the side that lost the battle. The *badri* lists. That the Prophet lived
another eight years and Mecca fell six years after Badr. The fifth (*khums*) as an administrative
innovation dated to this day. The fourteen Muslim graves and the fact that they are still shown.

**Contested points owned.** The chapter's overall source-critical position, stated in the body in one
disciplined paragraph and expanded in "A Note on the Sources". In notes: whether Badr can bear the
weight later tradition put on it; the *yawm al-furqan* identification; Watt's reading of the economic
motive and the objections to it; whether the *badri* lists inflate participation.

**First line.** `Abu Sufyan brought the caravan into Mecca and sold it.`

**Last line.** `Everything that followed in Arabia followed from a fight that began because two water-parties met at a well in the dark.`

**MUST NOT COVER.** Any new battle narrative. Uhud in more than three sentences. Qaynuqa' in more
than two. No new named episodes from the battle itself.

## 6. Style sheet

**Numbers.** Words up to and including one hundred (*three hundred and thirteen*, *seventy*,
*fourteen*); numerals above that only where a figure is being weighed as a figure (*950*, *4,000
dirhams*). Round estimates always flagged as round ("roughly", "about", "something on the order of").
Distances in miles with the Arabic measure given once in a note. Never a decimal.

**Dates.** Hijri first, Common Era in parentheses on first use in the chapter: *17 Ramadan 2 AH
(13 March 624)*. Thereafter *17 Ramadan* or *the seventeenth*. No "AD", no "CE" after the first
instance. Months of Ramadan given by day-of-month, not by weekday, except the one mention that the
day was a Friday.

**Honorifics.** *the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ* on first mention **in each section**, then *the Prophet* or
*Muhammad*. Companions plain: *Abu Bakr*, *'Umar*, *'Ali*. No *radiya Allah 'anhu*, no *(pbuh)*, no
*Sayyiduna*. Meccan opponents plain and without epithet: *Abu Jahl*, not "the arch-enemy". God is
*God*, not *Allah*, except inside a quotation.

**Endnote numbering.** Each section drafts its own notes as `[^s1-1]`, `[^s1-2]` … `[^s7-9]`, in
order of appearance, with definitions collected at the foot of that section's file. The merge pass
renumbers to `[^1]`…`[^80]` in reading order. No section may reference another section's note; if
two sections need the same citation, both write it out.

**Quotation.** Direct speech from the sources in double quotes with the attribution adjacent and the
citation in the note. Where a saying exists in several wordings, quote one and note the others. No
invented dialogue, ever; where the chapter needs a man's manner rather than his words, paraphrase in
indirect speech and say whose report it is.

**Paragraphs.** 90–160 words typical; a one-sentence paragraph is permitted once per section for
weight. No bullet lists in the chapter body. No sub-headings inside sections.

**Vocabulary bans.** morale, logistics, intelligence, assets, propaganda, PR, strategy in the modern
staff sense, "game-changer", "little did they know", "history would remember", "the rest is history".
Permitted and to be explained inline on first use: *wadi*, *mubaraza*, *blood-money* (*diya*),
*Muhajirun*, *Ansar*, *maghazi*, *isnad*, *khums*, *'arish*, *badri*.
