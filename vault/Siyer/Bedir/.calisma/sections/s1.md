## The Fodder of Yathrib

Abu Sufyan ibn Harb crouched at the water of Badr and broke a lump of camel dung apart in his fingers. There were date-stones in it. He stood up and said, to nobody in particular, that this was the fodder of Yathrib.[^s1-1]

He had been asking after riders all the way down the coast, at every well and of every man he met, and at Badr a local of Juhayna named Majdi ibn 'Amr had told him that two strangers had couched their camels on the rise above the water, filled a skin, and gone again. Majdi thought nothing of them. Abu Sufyan, who had spent the whole return leg expecting to be hunted, thought a great deal of them: dates were what a man of the Medinan oasis fed his beast, and a beast fed on Medinan dates had a Medinan rider. He turned the caravan off the inland road that night and drove it west, out onto the dry coastal strip between Badr and the sea, past the wells and past the men waiting at them.[^s1-2]

The two riders had come from Muhammad's town, and they had come looking for him.

Yathrib lay some two hundred and fifty miles north of Mecca: not a city in any sense a Roman or a Persian would have recognised, but a wide oasis of date-palms and barley plots and walled compounds strung along a basalt plain, each cluster with its own wells and its own armed men. Its Arab population was two large tribes, Aws and Khazraj, who had been fighting each other within living memory; three Jewish tribes farmed and traded there and held the best land in parts of it. Into this the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ had come in the late summer of 622, with something under a hundred Meccan families, at the invitation of men of Aws and Khazraj who wanted, among other things, an arbiter.

The Meccans who came with him were the Muhajirun, the emigrants — men and women who had left a mercantile city where they held property, credit and standing, and arrived in an agricultural one where they held none of these things and did not know how to farm. Those who took them in were the Ansar, the helpers: the Aws and Khazraj households that lodged them, married them, and in a number of cases split their palm-groves with them. What the emigrants had left behind did not stay behind. Houses in Mecca were occupied, debts written off in the debtor's favour, and goods and shares turned over to those who remained; Ibn Ishaq notes without comment that the house of one emigrant was sold by his own kin.[^s1-3] Capital in Mecca did not sit still. It went north with the caravans. A man in Medina in the spring of 624 could stand near enough to the road to watch, in the abstract at least, the proceeds of his own confiscated stock going past him as somebody else's cargo.

That road was the reason Mecca existed. The town had no crops and almost no water; it lived on the sanctuary, on the pilgrim traffic, and above all on the two-way carrying trade between the Yemen and Syria — hides, leather, raisins, silver, incense going north; textiles, oil, worked metal, weapons and wheat coming south. A single camel carried something like three hundred pounds, and a load of Syrian cloth or plate might be worth what an ordinary man could earn in years. The trade was not incidental to Quraysh; it was the substance of the place, financed by shares taken up across the clans, so that a caravan of any size on that road belonged, in fractions, to nearly everybody.[^s1-4]

Medina's position with respect to that road was the whole of its leverage. The coastal trunk route from Mecca to Syria ran up the Hijaz west of the mountains, and at its nearest — in the neighbourhood of Badr — it passed within about eighty miles of the oasis: three or four days out for a party of riders, near enough to reach the road, take what was on it, and be home inside a fortnight.[^s1-5] Whether that leverage was ever exercised as a deliberate squeeze on Meccan food is a question modern historians have argued more sharply than the sources do, but the Meccans themselves plainly understood what the oasis was now sitting beside.[^s1-6]

None of this was new in Ramadan of 2 AH. For something over a year small parties had been going out from Medina towards the road and coming back with nothing. In the autumn the Prophet himself led men to Waddan, near al-Abwa', and found no caravan; in the following month Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib or 'Ubayda ibn al-Harith — the reports vary as to which expedition was whose — went towards the coast; in the spring there was Buwat, and after it al-'Ushayra in the valley of Yanbu', where the Prophet took some hundred and fifty men down to the sea and arrived days after Abu Sufyan's caravan had gone through on its way north. That was the same cargo, missed on its outward leg. Then in Safar a Fihri raider named Kurz ibn Jabir lifted grazing camels from the pastures outside Medina and was chased as far as Safawan, in the country of Badr, and lost. Five approaches to the same stretch of road in a year, and not one of them had produced a fight.[^s1-7]

One had produced a death. In Rajab, seven or eight men under 'Abdallah ibn Jahsh — sent out with sealed orders, on the reports, and told not to open them for two days — came upon a small Qurayshi caravan at Nakhla, between Mecca and al-Ta'if, carrying raisins, leather and wine. They took it, and in taking it they shot 'Amr ibn al-Hadrami dead. The trouble was the month. Rajab was one of the sacred months in which Arab custom forbade fighting, and Mecca had a genuine grievance to broadcast: that Muhammad's men had shed blood in a truce month at the edge of the sanctuary. Ibn Ishaq reports that the Prophet withheld the spoils and the prisoners at first, and was displeased with the raiders.[^s1-8] The Hadrami blood-claim did not go away. It was still unpaid when Quraysh next had occasion to count it up, and it would count then.

Abu Sufyan's caravan left Gaza for the south some time in the middle weeks of the winter, a thousand camels or thereabouts under the charge of perhaps thirty or forty armed men, carrying the pooled trading capital of the Meccan houses.[^s1-9] It was a merchant's escort and not a war-party; the caravan's protection was speed and the choice of road. A loaded caravan on good ground made perhaps twenty-five miles in a day, which put the Badr latitude — the last open stretch before the road bent inland for Mecca — somewhere in the second week of Ramadan.

Medina knew roughly when to expect it, and the Prophet sent two of the emigrants north to sit on the road and make sure: Talha ibn 'Ubaydallah and Sa'id ibn Zayd, who went up to the neighbourhood of al-Hawra' on the coast and lay up there with a man of Juhayna until the caravan came down past them. Then they turned their camels and rode for Medina with the news that Abu Sufyan was on the road and coming south.[^s1-10]

It was Ramadan, the month of the fast, when a man ate and drank nothing between first light and sunset; the Muslims of Medina had been keeping it for a little over a year, and they would fight in it. Ramadan of that year fell in March. The heat was not yet the killing heat of high summer, but the winter rains were finished or nearly so, the grazing was drying off, and the wells along the road were at the level where a large body of men and animals could water at only a few of them. The oasis itself could put very little into the field in the way of equipment. When the count was made there were two horses in the whole force.

Two horses, and a fortnight's food, and a march to a stretch of open country where a thousand camels would be strung out over a mile of road under thirty or forty guards. What was being got ready in Medina was an interception, of the kind that had been attempted four or five times already and had four or five times come to nothing — a raid on property, mounted against men who were not expecting to be fought and who could be expected, if they were caught at a disadvantage, to give up the cargo and save themselves. Nothing in the preparations answers to an army. The tradition itself, which had every reason afterwards to present the day as appointed from the beginning, does not pretend otherwise: the Qur'an would remind the believers that what they had wanted was the easier of two things, and Ibn Ishaq's account of the muster is an account of men called out for a caravan.[^s1-11] What they would meet in the valley of Badr twelve days later nobody in the oasis had in mind that afternoon, because it did not yet exist as anybody's plan, in Medina or in Mecca.

The order went round Medina that afternoon, and it was not an order to prepare for a battle.

### Notes

[^s1-1]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the raid on the caravan of Quraysh; the wording of Abu Sufyan's exclamation is usually given as "By God, this is the fodder of Yathrib." al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, has the same episode with Majdi ibn 'Amr al-Juhani present at the well. The detail is small, circumstantial and to nobody's theological advantage — Quraysh's caravan-master is made to look competent by it — and it is the kind of report that survives because someone remembered it rather than because it proved anything. Date-stones in the dung: dates were standard camel fodder in the Medinan oasis and were not fed to beasts on the Meccan road, which is what makes the inference work.
[^s1-2]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the raid on the caravan of Quraysh; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, is fuller on the forced night march down the coastal strip without a watering halt. On the sources' testimony Abu Sufyan had been questioning travellers about Medina at every stage of the return, having been hunted on the outward leg at al-'Ushayra; see note 7 below.
[^s1-3]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the chapters on the Emigration; Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, i and iii (the biographies of individual emigrants), where the loss of Meccan property recurs as a formula in the notices of the Muhajirun. The general fact of confiscation is not in dispute in the tradition; the individual cases are told in family-tradition mode and the details of who took what should be held loosely.
[^s1-4]: On the camel-load: the standard Arabian pack-load of the period is usually reckoned at something between two hundred and fifty and three hundred and fifty pounds, and the figure in the text is a round middle. On the shareholding: Ibn Hisham makes the point that there was scarcely a man or woman of Quraysh with ten dinars to invest who had nothing in this caravan — the observation matters for s2, since it explains why the alarm produced a general muster rather than a rescue party.
[^s1-5]: Distances through this chapter are given in miles. The Arabic sources reckon in the *barid* (about twelve miles) and the day's march; Badr is placed by the geographers at four *barid* or thereabouts from the coast and something over a hundred miles from Medina by the road, which is longer than the straight line because the road bends. Eighty miles is the straight-line order of magnitude between the oasis and the nearest point of the caravan route, not a road distance.
[^s1-6]: Fred M. Donner, "Mecca's Food Supplies and Muhammad's Boycott," argues that Medina's expeditions towards the coast road were aimed at Mecca's grain supply from Syria and constituted an economic blockade, and that this, rather than the recovery of confiscated property or a general state of war, is the frame in which the raids of 1–2 AH make sense. W. Montgomery Watt, *Muhammad at Medina*, ch. 1, reads the raids as the ordinary practice of a new polity establishing itself by *ghazw*, with the Meccan trade the obvious object because it was the only thing worth taking. The two readings are not exclusive. The sources themselves offer no economic argument at all: they present the expeditions as attempts to come at Qurayshi property, and the chapter follows them in that, while noting that the Meccan reaction — a muster of the whole town — is more intelligible if Quraysh believed the road itself was at stake.
[^s1-7]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the sections on the raids preceding Badr; Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids), which gives the fullest list and the dates by month; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, the expeditions before Badr. The lists do not agree in order or in leadership — Hamza ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib, 'Ubayda ibn al-Harith and Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas are variously credited with the earliest of the sea-coast parties — and the discrepancies are the ordinary kind produced by clan traditions each claiming a first. Whether these expeditions were raids that failed or reconnaissance that succeeded is a real question. Their small size (some as few as eight men), their instructions in some reports to observe rather than engage, and the fact that they repeatedly reached the road just after a caravan had passed, all suggest that the earliest of them were probing the route and its rhythm; but Waddan and al-'Ushayra were led by the Prophet in person with a hundred and fifty to two hundred men and cannot be anything but attempts at interception. The reasonable reading is a progression: patrols first, interceptions after, with the knowledge gathered by the first making the second possible. The pursuit of Kurz ibn Jabir to Safawan has a further consequence for the battle — it took the Prophet and a core of his companions over the ground at Badr months before they had to fight on it.
[^s1-8]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the raid of 'Abdallah ibn Jahsh at Nakhla; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, the raid of Nakhla; Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids). The number of the party is given as eight or as twelve; the sealed-orders detail is in Ibn Ishaq and is repeated by everyone after him. Q 2:217 ("They ask you about the sacred month, about fighting in it") is referred by the exegetes to this episode, which is itself evidence that the Meccan complaint had to be answered. The victim's name is 'Amr ibn al-Hadrami; his brother 'Amir survived him and is heard from again in s3.
[^s1-9]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the raid on the caravan of Quraysh, gives a thousand camels and thirty men; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, has forty men and in one place a smaller figure for the animals, and the "seven hundred camels" of some later retellings looks like contamination from the count of the Meccan army's own camels, for which see s3. The valuation — fifty thousand dinars, or in some reports fifty thousand *mithqals* of gold — should be read as a round number meaning "an enormous sum" rather than as an accounting figure; it appears in the sources in exactly the places where a storyteller needs to convey scale, and there is no plausible chain by which a Medinan tradition would have known the caravan's books. A thousand camels is the figure this chapter follows, with the caution that it too is round. What is not in doubt is the ratio: a very large cargo under a very small guard.
[^s1-10]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the raid on the caravan of Quraysh; the two watchers are Talha ibn 'Ubaydallah and Sa'id ibn Zayd, and Ibn Ishaq has them sheltered at al-Hawra' by Kashd al-Juhani. Their absence from Medina on this errand is the reason both men were away from the battle itself — a detail the tradition preserves against its own interest, since both were later of the highest standing and a fabricator would have put them in the valley.
[^s1-11]: Q 8:5–7, revealed after the battle and looking back at the expedition's object, is the earliest layer of evidence on this point and is handled at length in s2. Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the raid on the caravan of Quraysh, describes the muster in Medina as a call to go out against Abu Sufyan's caravan and nothing else; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, is the same. al-Mubarakpuri, *Ar-Raheeq Al-Makhtum*, "The Battle of Badr," reads the expedition as an interception that turned into a battle, which is also the reading of Watt, *Muhammad at Medina*, ch. 1.
