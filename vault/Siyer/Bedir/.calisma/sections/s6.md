## The Pit and the Prisoners

The pit was an old well on the floor of the wadi that had fallen in and gone dry. Nobody dug a grave for the Quraysh. In the late afternoon, with the heat coming off the sand, the men of Medina took the bodies of the chiefs by the feet and the wrists, dragged them across to the collapsed shaft, put them in, and levelled the sand over the mouth of it.[^s6-1] Ibn Ishaq names twenty-four of the dead as going into that hole, which is the figure to prefer: one fallen well will not take seventy men, and the rest were most probably covered where they lay. The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ stood at the lip of it as the light went and spoke to them by name — "O 'Utba ibn Rabi'a, O Shayba ibn Rabi'a, O Umayya ibn Khalaf, O Abu Jahl ibn Hisham" — and asked them whether they had found what their lord promised them to be true, as he and his men had found what theirs had promised. 'Umar asked him why he was talking to bodies that could not hear. He answered that they heard him no worse than 'Umar did, but could not answer.[^s6-2]

The count, when it was made, was about seventy of the Quraysh killed and about seventy taken alive, and fourteen of the Muslims dead, six of the Muhajirun and eight of the Ansar. Seventy is a round and satisfying number and the name-lists do not quite reach it either way; but the figures are old, and consistent across reporters who disagree about almost everything else on that field.[^s6-3] The fourteen were buried on the eastern edge of the plain, near where their line had stood, and their graves are still shown there.

'Utba ibn Rabi'a was the hardest body to move. He had been a heavy man in his sixties and the party dragging him stalled in the soft sand. His son Abu Hudhayfa ibn 'Utba, who had gone over to Muhammad years before and had stood that morning in the Muslim line, watched his father hauled to the well and could not keep his face. Ibn Ishaq has the Prophet notice it and ask him whether something had entered his heart about his father, and has Abu Hudhayfa answer that he did not doubt about his father or his death, but that he had known judgement and forbearance and virtue in him and had hoped these would bring him to Islam, and that seeing what had come upon him instead grieved him. He was spoken to gently and prayed for.[^s6-4] He was not the only one: 'Umar had killed his own mother's brother, Abu 'Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah had been pursued through the fighting by his own father, Abu Bakr's son 'Abd al-Rahman had spent the day on the Meccan side, and Mus'ab ibn 'Umayr, passing his brother Abu 'Aziz roped in the prisoners' column, told the Ansari holding him to bind the man tighter, because his mother had money and would pay.

The spoils nearly did what the Quraysh had not. A hundred and fifty camels, ten horses, and heaps of mail, helmets, swords, spears and waterskins came into the camp, and three sets of men claimed them: those who had held the line, those who had gone out over the sand after the runners, and those who had stood all day around the shelter on the rise. The argument turned bad quickly, between men who had been shoulder to shoulder an hour earlier. The opening verse of Surat al-Anfal took the question out of their hands — the spoils belonged to God and to the Messenger, and the believers were to set right the relations between them — and the Prophet had every piece brought out of every private hand into one heap, put an Ansari, 'Abdallah ibn Ka'b al-Mazini, in charge of counting it, set aside a fifth, and divided the rest into equal shares, foot and mounted, Muhajir and Ansar, with the shares of the fourteen going to their heirs.[^s6-5] The fifth — the khums, God's portion, in practice the portion the Prophet administered for the poor and for public need — was fixed at Badr and stayed fixed.

They spent three days in the valley, because the wounded had to be fit to travel and seventy prisoners had to be roped and fed, and then went up the road north-east towards Medina. Two men did not finish the journey. At al-Safra', a valley of palms a day's march out of Badr, al-Nadr ibn al-Harith was brought out of the column and 'Ali ibn Abi Talib killed him. Al-Nadr had carried the Quraysh banner, and in Mecca he used to sit down after the Prophet had recited in the precinct of the Ka'ba and tell the Persian stories of Rustam and Isfandiyar, to show that anyone could make tales.[^s6-6] Three days later, at a place called 'Irq al-Zabya between al-Rawha' and Medina, 'Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt was brought out in the same way, and 'Asim ibn Thabit killed him, on Ibn Ishaq's account, which I follow.[^s6-7] 'Uqba had thrown filth on the Prophet while he prayed in Mecca and had once wound a cloak round his neck from behind and pulled. He asked why he alone of all the Quraysh, and was told, for his enmity to God and His Messenger. Ibn Ishaq gives him one more question — who would look after his sons — and one more answer: the Fire. These two were the only prisoners put to death out of about seventy; the rest watched, and walked on north.

From al-Rawha' the Prophet sent two riders ahead with the news, 'Abdallah ibn Rawaha to the upper quarters of the town and Zayd ibn Haritha to the lower, Zayd on the Prophet's own she-camel al-Qaswa' so that nobody would take him for a fugitive. Medina had been three days on rumour. What the boys running out along the road wanted first was the roll of the dead, and second whether it was true that Abu Jahl was in a hole in the ground at Badr.[^s6-8]

What to do with seventy men was a harder question than what to do with the camels. Nothing in the usage of the day settled it: a raiding party ransomed its captives or killed them as it judged, and there was no rule yet for an army that fought on God's behalf. Muslim preserves a long report on the argument, and the tradition has settled into a very tidy shape, in which the Prophet asked his two closest men and got opposite counsel. Abu Bakr said they were kinsmen and cousins, that a ransom would be a strength against the enemy, and that God might yet guide some of them. 'Umar said that they were the men who had driven the Muslims out of Mecca and called them liars, that their necks should be struck, and — in the version he is himself made to tell — that each man should be handed his own relative to kill, 'Ali given his brother 'Aqil and Hamza his brother al-'Abbas.[^s6-9] The ransom was taken. Then Surat al-Anfal rebuked the taking of it: that no prophet should have prisoners until he had made slaughter in the land, that the believers wanted the goods of this world and God wanted the hereafter, and that had a decree not gone out already, a painful punishment would have followed. The same verses declared the money lawful to eat. 'Umar is reported to have said that he and the Prophet wept.[^s6-10]

The ransoms ran from a thousand dirhams for a poor man to 4,000 for a chief, graded by what a family could be expected to find, and Mecca sent the money north household by household, up the same road the army had come down. Some prisoners had nothing. Those of them who could read and write were told they might buy their freedom by teaching ten boys of Medina to write; when the boy could write, the teacher went home. One of those boys was Zayd ibn Thabit, who became the Prophet's scribe and, twenty years later, the man who collected the Qur'an. Suhayl ibn 'Amr, an orator whose mouth was his trade, drew from 'Umar a proposal that his lower front teeth be pulled so that he could never make a speech against the Muslims again; the Prophet refused to mutilate a man, and said that Suhayl might yet stand somewhere that would please him.[^s6-11]

Among the prisoners was the Prophet's uncle. Al-'Abbas ibn 'Abd al-Muttalib had come out with the Quraysh, was taken by Abu al-Yasar Ka'b ibn 'Amr, a small man, which was remarked on all the way to Medina, and was ransomed like anyone else — for himself, for his two nephews and for an ally — out of gold he had left behind with his wife Umm al-Fadl, which he had not mentioned until he was told about it. He asked to have the sum counted against the gold already taken from him on the field, and was told that was God's and the Muslims'. That is the plain shape of it, and the later story that he had marched under compulsion, or had been a Muslim in secret all along, will not carry the weight put on it; the family that ruled the caliphate for five centuries had every reason to want its ancestor at Badr on the right side of the wadi.[^s6-12]

Abu'l-'As ibn al-Rabi' was taken too — a merchant of 'Abd Shams, a nephew of Khadija, and husband of the Prophet's daughter Zaynab bint Muhammad, who was still in Mecca. His brother 'Amr brought the ransom, and with it a necklace of Zaynab's, onyx from Zafar, which her mother Khadija had given her on her wedding day and which she had now sent north to buy her husband. The Prophet asked his men whether they would let her have it back; they gave it up, and Abu'l-'As went home with the necklace and a private undertaking to send Zaynab to her father. He kept it. He became Muslim years afterwards and she was returned to him.

In Mecca the news came in on the third day, brought by a rider of Khuza'a, al-Haysuman ibn 'Abdallah, who came into the valley and simply began reciting the dead: 'Utba ibn Rabi'a, Shayba ibn Rabi'a, Abu Jahl ibn Hisham, Umayya ibn Khalaf, Zam'a ibn al-Aswad, Nubayh and Munabbih the sons of al-Hajjaj. Safwan ibn Umayya, sitting in the Hijr beside the Ka'ba, said that if the man still had his wits they should ask him about Safwan; they asked, and al-Haysuman said that Safwan was sitting in the Hijr, and that he had seen his father and his brother killed. He was believed after that.[^s6-13]

Abu Lahab had not gone to Badr; he had hired al-'As ibn Hisham to go in his place against a debt, and he now sat by the Zamzam tent and waited for a witness. Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith, the Prophet's own cousin and still then an opponent, came in from the field and was called over — come here, nephew, you have the news — and made to sit and tell it in front of the men. He told them the army had broken at the first shock, that they had been killed and taken as the Muslims pleased, and that he did not blame the Quraysh for it, because they had met men in white on piebald horses between heaven and earth, and nothing stood before them. A freedman of al-'Abbas named Abu Rafi', sitting nearby trimming arrow shafts, said gladly that those had been the angels. Abu Lahab knocked him down. Umm al-Fadl, al-'Abbas's wife, picked up a tent-pole and opened Abu Lahab's head with it, and told him he bullied the man because his master was away. The wound went bad. Abu Lahab was dead within the week, of a pustular sickness the Meccans took for plague, and his sons would not touch the body; it was pushed into a hollow at the edge of the town and stoned over from a distance.

Al-Aswad ibn al-Muttalib had lost three sons in the wadi and had gone blind, Ibn Ishaq says, from weeping for them. Hearing a woman wailing somewhere in the town one night, he sent his slave out to learn whether the mourning had been permitted at last, so that he might weep for his own; the boy came back and said she was crying over a lost camel. In Mecca no woman was allowed to weep where she could be heard.

### Notes

[^s6-1]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the burial of the Quraysh dead at Badr; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr. The word used is *qalib*, an unlined water-pit or a well that has lost its lining — not a grave and not a cistern, and the distinction matters, because it tells us the dead of Quraysh were disposed of in a hole that already existed. Al-Waqidi has the bodies dragged by work parties and covered over; Bukhari's material on the episode, in Kitab al-Maghazi, bab of the killing of Abu Jahl and the following babs, refers to the pit as already there.

[^s6-2]: Bukhari, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Maghazi, bab of the killing of Abu Jahl, and Kitab al-Jana'iz; Muslim, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Janna, on the dead of Badr in the pit; Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the same passage. The two families of report differ in wording: Ibn Ishaq has the Prophet call the dead by name and ask whether they have found what their lord promised true, and has him say that God had promised him victory over them; Bukhari's version is shorter and turns on the exchange with 'Umar. 'A'isha objected to the report on doctrinal grounds, holding that what the Prophet said was "they now know that what I used to tell them was the truth," and citing Q 27:80 and 35:22, that the dead cannot be made to hear. Her objection is preserved in Bukhari and in Muslim and is worth keeping precisely because it is early: it shows a first-generation Muslim scholar arguing about the theology of a report inside living memory of the event, which is the strongest evidence we have that the transmitters were not simply inventing. Ibn Kathir, *al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya*, iii, canvasses the versions and prefers the fuller one; the chapter reports both and adjudicates neither.

[^s6-3]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the lists of the Quraysh slain and of the prisoners at Badr, and the list of the Muslim dead, gives seventy killed and seventy taken; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, names his dead and prisoners clan by clan and the totals recoverable from his lists fall a little short of seventy in each column, as do those in Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids). Q 3:165 and 8:67 are consistent with heavy Meccan losses but give no figures. The candid position: seventy is a traditional round number of the kind Arabic historiography reaches for, and the name-lists were compiled later and are demonstrably incomplete, so what can be said is that something on the order of sixty to eighty Meccans were killed and a comparable number taken. On the Muslim side the fourteen — six Muhajirun, eight Ansar — is the figure of Ibn Ishaq and of most later writers; al-Waqidi's lists and some of Ibn Sa'd's material push towards twenty-two by including men who died later of wounds, among them 'Ubayda ibn al-Harith, buried at al-Safra' on the road home. Fourteen is the count of the men left in the ground at Badr, and this chapter uses it in that sense.

[^s6-4]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of Abu Hudhayfa at the pit; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, adds the detail of 'Utba's weight and of the party stalling in the sand. The Rabi'a-family material at Badr — 'Utba's attempt at peace, his son's grief, his daughter Hind's later hatred — is unusually full, and one might suspect a family tradition; but the reports as they stand do not flatter 'Utba, and a family shaping its own memory does not leave its patriarch heavy, doubted and dragged.

[^s6-5]: Q 8:1 for the spoils and Q 8:41 for the fifth; Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the division of the spoils of Badr; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, is the fullest on the three claimant parties and on the appointment of 'Abdallah ibn Ka'b al-Mazini; Bukhari, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Khums. The animal figures — a hundred and fifty camels and ten horses — are al-Waqidi's and are round; they are also, at roughly a fifth of the Meccan train, plausible for what a beaten army abandons. On the equal share and the shares of the absent-on-duty and of the dead, Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids). Watt, *Muhammad at Medina*, ch. 7, treats the Badr division as the origin of the later law of *ghanima*, and the sequence in the sources supports him: an improvised ruling on a field, later generalised.

[^s6-6]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the march back from Badr and the killing of al-Nadr ibn al-Harith; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr. Ibn Ishaq names 'Ali as the executioner; a minority of reports leaves the man unnamed or gives al-Miqdad ibn 'Amr, who had claimed al-Nadr as his own prisoner and, in one report, complained of losing the ransom. The Rustam and Isfandiyar material — al-Nadr competing with the Qur'an by reciting Persian romance — belongs to the Meccan chapters of Ibn Ishaq and to the commentary tradition on Q 31:6 and 83:13; it is given here because it explains why he was singled out.

[^s6-7]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the killing of 'Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt at 'Irq al-Zabya, names 'Asim ibn Thabit al-Ansari, and this is the report the chapter follows: it is the earliest, and it keeps the two executions distinct in place, date and executioner, which a later harmoniser would have had no motive to do. A well-attested variant gives 'Ali ibn Abi Talib for both men, and it is easy to see how that came about — 'Ali's name at al-Safra' drawing 'Uqba's death to it as well, with some assistance from later Shi'i and proto-Shi'i emphasis on 'Ali's role at Badr. Al-Waqidi and al-Tabari, *Tarikh*, the year 2 AH, both carry the doubling. The exchange over 'Uqba's sons, and the answer "the Fire," is Ibn Ishaq's; some later writers omit it, and it is not hard to see why.

[^s6-8]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the sending of the two heralds; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, has 'Abdallah ibn Rawaha to the upper town and Zayd ibn Haritha to the lower on al-Qaswa'. Al-Waqidi also preserves the report that Ruqayya, the Prophet's daughter, was buried in Medina on the day Zayd rode in.

[^s6-9]: Muslim, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Jihad wa'l-Siyar, the bab on the prisoners of Badr, preserves the long report in 'Umar's own voice, including the proposal that each man be given his own kinsman to kill; Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, and al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, both carry the Abu Bakr / 'Umar exchange, al-Waqidi with more speakers, including 'Abdallah ibn Rawaha, who is made to propose burning the prisoners in a fire. The shape of the story is suspiciously good: two counsellors, mercy against severity, the Prophet choosing mercy and revelation siding with severity. That is how a tradition remembers an argument, not how an argument happens. What is likely historical is that there was a real dispute in the camp and on the road, that ransom was the outcome, that it was thought afterwards to have been the wrong outcome, and that the positions were later sorted into the two men who had come to stand for leniency and rigour in the community's memory of itself. The chapter accordingly gives the debate as the sources give it and marks the tidiness rather than pretending to see behind it.

[^s6-10]: Q 8:67–69. On the connection of these verses to the ransom, see the commentary tradition summarised in Ibn Kathir, *al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya*, iii, and his *al-Sira al-Nabawiyya*; the report of the Prophet and 'Umar weeping is in Muslim, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Jihad wa'l-Siyar. The rebuke is early: a community shaping its memory freely does not invent a revelation criticising its founder's judgement, and Q 8:67–69 is one of the strongest internal arguments for the Qur'an as the oldest layer of the Badr material.

[^s6-11]: On the graded ransoms, al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, and Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids); the top rate of 4,000 dirhams and the bottom of a thousand are al-Waqidi's, with intermediate rates by clan. Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, has the release without ransom of Abu 'Azza the poet and of al-Muttalib ibn Hantab. The teaching arrangement — a literate prisoner freed for teaching ten boys of Medina to write — is reported by Ibn Sa'd and by al-Baladhuri, and Zayd ibn Thabit is named among the pupils; the report is late enough to warrant caution about the round ten, but the practice fits an unlettered town that had just acquired seventy educated Meccans and no money to buy anything else with. On Suhayl ibn 'Amr and the teeth, Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, and Ibn Kathir, *al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya*, iii; the saying that Suhayl might yet take a stand that would please the Prophet is read by later writers against his speech at Mecca on the day the Prophet died, and the report may have been shaped to fit it.

[^s6-12]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of al-'Abbas's capture and ransom; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, on the gold left with Umm al-Fadl; Bukhari, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Maghazi, bab of the Badr expedition, and Kitab al-Khums, for the ransom of al-'Abbas along with 'Aqil ibn Abi Talib, Nawfal ibn al-Harith and his ally 'Utba ibn 'Amr. Three positions circulate in the sources. The first, which this chapter takes, is that he came out with the Quraysh as a Hashimite of standing did, fought or at least stood in their line, was taken, and was ransomed on the same terms as anyone of his rank. The second is that he was brought out under compulsion, unwilling, and that the Prophet had told his men before the battle not to kill him. The third is that he had been a Muslim in secret since before Badr and had been sending word from Mecca. The second and third grew in the telling, and it is not difficult to see whose interest they served: the 'Abbasid caliphs traced their claim through this man, and it was intolerable to their apologists that the ancestor should have stood against the Prophet with a sword. Against them stands the ransom itself, exacted in full and with the ledger of hidden gold produced against him; and the answer he is given in the earliest report — that if what he says is true God will reward him for it, but that outwardly he was against them — is precisely the sentence a later apologist would not have needed to preserve. The instruction not to kill him is likely enough as a kinsman's instruction; that is a different thing from compulsion. See also Watt, *Muhammad at Medina*, on the shaping of Hashimite and 'Abbasid material in the sira.

[^s6-13]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the news reaching Mecca, is the source for al-Haysuman ibn 'Abdallah, for Safwan in the Hijr, for Abu Lahab at Zamzam, for Abu Sufyan ibn al-Harith's report of men in white on piebald horses, and for Umm al-Fadl and the tent-pole; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, adds detail on Abu Lahab's death from *al-'adasa*, the "lentil" pustule the Meccans took for plague, and on the stoning of the body from a distance. On the prohibition of public mourning and on al-Aswad ibn al-Muttalib's blindness and the woman weeping for her camel, Ibn Ishaq again; the anecdote is the kind of thing that survives because it was told as a poem's occasion, and al-Aswad's verses on his sons are quoted by Ibn Hisham in the same place.
