## Above the Necks

The Muslims did not move. The order had gone down the line before the Quraysh were within a bowshot, and it was an order about restraint rather than about attack: keep the ranks, shoot if they press close, and do not draw a sword until they are upon you.[^s5-1] Ibn Ishaq gives it in that form, and it has the shape of something remembered rather than composed, because it is tactically unglamorous and it fits the ground. The three hundred and thirteen stood on the firm, rain-packed sand of the eastern side of the wadi — the flood-channel that ran down the valley, dry in March but for the wells sunk in its bed — with the water behind them. The Quraysh had to come to them across the churned soft going at the foot of al-'Aqanqal. Every minute the Muslim line held was a minute the larger army spent walking uphill in sand with the sun coming up in its eyes.

So the first phase of Badr was not a charge but an exchange of arrows across a closing gap, and the first Muslims to die at Badr died of it. Mihja', a freedman of 'Umar ibn al-Khattab, was hit and killed before the lines touched; al-Waqidi, who is fullest on the ground at Badr, puts Harith ibn Suraqa of the Ansar at the cistern behind the line, struck through while he drank.[^s5-2] Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas shot from the second rank until his own arrows were gone, and then gathered spent Qurayshi shafts out of the sand and shot them back.

It is worth being plain here about what these men were fighting with, because it governs everything that followed. Between the whole Muslim army there were six coats of mail and eight swords.[^s5-3] The rest fought with spears, with bows, with stones out of the wadi bed, and with their hands. A sword at Badr was the property of a rich man or a lucky one; when Abu Dujana bound a red cloth about his head and went forward out of the centre-right, the display was not a swordsman's swagger over lesser weapons but a signal of intent, understood by everyone who saw it, that he did not mean to come back. The Quraysh, by contrast, had come out equipped as an army: mail, helmets, a hundred horses, and coats of plate on the men in the middle of their line.

Ibn Ishaq reports that when the two masses were near enough for the dust of the Qurayshi advance to be blowing across the gap, the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ came down from the shelter of palm-branches on the rise behind the line, took up a handful of the valley's gravel and dust, and threw it towards the Quraysh, saying: "Let the faces be disfigured."[^s5-4] The report goes on that the dust went into their eyes and mouths along the whole front, and that the advance staggered. Then came the word the line had been waiting for, and the three hundred went forward off the hard ground at a run, calling out the single word "One! One!" — and the two armies met near the middle of the valley floor.

What the Qur'an tells the believers about the next hour is not a description of it. Surat al-Anfal speaks to them of a thousand of the angels, rank on rank, sent down as help and as a token, and of a command given over their heads: "I am with you, so strengthen those who believe. I shall cast terror into the hearts of those who disbelieve; so strike above the necks, and strike off their fingertips."[^s5-5] It is allusive, addressed to men who had been there and did not need the scene set. The narrative sources, coming a century and more later, filled it in. Ibn Ishaq preserves a report from an Ansari of the Banu Mazin who said that he was chasing a man of the Quraysh in front of him when he heard, above the noise, the crack of a whip and a rider's voice crying "Forward, Hayzum!" — and the man he was chasing fell dead before he reached him; when he told the Prophet, he was told that this was help from the third heaven. Muslim has the same account by way of Ibn 'Abbas.[^s5-6] Al-Waqidi collects others: men who found their strongest opponents going down to strokes from nowhere, heads falling with no swordsman behind them, and a sound in the air like stones dropped into a brass basin. In the later material the help acquires faces, names and uniforms — angels in the likeness of al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam, who wore a yellow turban that day, so that the sign of the help was his headgear.[^s5-7] That growth is itself instructive, and the reports are set out in the notes for what they are: what men of the next generations said the men of Badr had said they heard.

The dust is also the reason no detailed account of the middle of the battle can be trusted. Some thirteen hundred men, seven hundred camels and a hundred horses were trampling a dry valley floor that had been rained on the night before and had dried by mid-morning; within minutes of the lines meeting, nobody on the field could see more than the men immediately around him. A melee of that kind has no shape to recover. What the sources preserve instead — and preserve very well, because each of them came down in the family of the man it concerned — is a set of episodes: this man, on this piece of ground, doing this. The honest way to give the battle is to move through them.

'Ukkasha ibn Mihsan, of the Muhajirun, fought until his sword broke off in his hand. He went back through the ranks to the Prophet, who gave him a dry palm-branch and told him to fight with that; and Ibn Ishaq reports that when he shook it, it became a long white blade, and that he went back into the press with it and it opened the line in front of him. He kept it, and called it al-'Awn, the Aid, and carried it in every battle afterwards until he was killed years later in the wars of the apostasy; and the sword stayed in his family for generations after that.[^s5-8]

Abu Jahl — 'Amr ibn Hisham of the Banu Makhzum, who had shamed the Meccan column forward when it was already turning round — fought where a Qurayshi chief was expected to fight, mounted and armoured in the middle of his own clan's contingent, inside a ring of picked men of Makhzum. It was the strongest single point in the Qurayshi array and, once the wings began to spill outward, the last part of it still standing. The men who broke into that ring were not Muhajirun of the Prophet's own city-band nor famous swordsmen but two young men of the Ansar, of Medinan farming clans who had known Abu Jahl only as a name.

Mu'adh ibn 'Amr ibn al-Jamuh, of the Banu Salama, got through the guard and struck at him, and the blow took Abu Jahl's leg off at the middle of the shin. Mu'adh described it afterwards in the only terms a man of Medina had ready: the leg flew off, he said, like a date-stone spat out from under the crusher. Abu Jahl came down out of the saddle. His son 'Ikrima wheeled in on Mu'adh and cut him across the shoulder so that his arm hung by the skin at his side. Mu'adh fought on with it swinging, then — because it was in his way — put his foot on it and pulled until it tore free, and went on fighting for the rest of the day. He lived, and lived long, and it is from him that the account comes.[^s5-9] Then Mu'awwidh ibn 'Afra came over the fallen man and struck him again where he lay, and left him motionless but still breathing, and went on into the press and was killed there, deeper inside the enemy line than any other Muslim who fell that day.

On the Qurayshi left, 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf came upon Umayya ibn Khalaf of the Banu Jumah, a fat man in his own mail, with his son beside him. The two had been friends in Mecca — trading partners, on the better reports, in the years before the preaching — and Umayya asked to be taken prisoner, since a prisoner could be ransomed and a corpse could not. 'Abd al-Rahman took the two of them and started them back towards the Muslim rear. Bilal saw them going. Umayya had owned Bilal in Mecca and had staked him out in the sun on the hot stones of the Batha to make him renounce, and what Bilal shouted, in Ibn Ishaq's wording, was: "Umayya ibn Khalaf! The head of unbelief! I shall not live if he lives." 'Abd al-Rahman said that they were his captives. Bilal called to the Ansar, and a knot of them closed in, and 'Abd al-Rahman threw his own body across Umayya to keep them off and was cut for it; they killed the son first and then the father under his hands, and a stroke aimed at Umayya's leg took part of 'Abd al-Rahman's foot as well. Ibn Ishaq has him say afterwards, without much charity: "God forgive Bilal. He has lost me my prisoners and my armour."[^s5-10]

The sources cannot get through this hour without noticing the relatives. Abu 'Ubayda ibn al-Jarrah, whom men later remembered chiefly as an administrator, killed his own father al-Jarrah in the press — a report the exegetes attached to the verse about believers who do not take their fathers' side.[^s5-11] Hamza killed Tu'ayma ibn 'Adi, whose kin would remember it at Uhud. 'Umar met his mother's brother, al-'As ibn Hisham of Makhzum, and killed him. Abu Hudhayfa fought forward past the bodies of his father 'Utba, his brother al-Walid and his uncle Shayba without turning aside. Abu Bakr's son 'Abd al-Rahman, who was in the Qurayshi ranks and did not become a Muslim for years, told his father long afterwards that he had had him in his sights that day and let him pass; Abu Bakr said that he would not have done the same.[^s5-12] Hanzala, a son of Abu Sufyan, was killed somewhere in the Qurayshi centre. 'Amir ibn al-Hadrami, who had rejected the blood-money offered to close his brother's case and get the army home, was killed on the field he had insisted on.

Then both ends of the Qurayshi line let go — the right first, the wing that had lost 'Utba and Shayba and al-Walid before a blow was struck in the general fight, and the left within minutes of Abu Jahl going down. The standard came down. The centre was squeezed back against its own baggage at the foot of the dune, and after that it was not a battle but a climb: men throwing off mail to get up the soft slope of al-'Aqanqal that had already cost them the morning, horses foundering, seven hundred camels left standing where they were tethered.

As the field emptied the Prophet sent men through the fallen to find out whether Abu Jahl was among them. 'Abdallah ibn Mas'ud found him lying in the churned sand, still alive, and set his foot on his neck. Bukhari preserves the exchange in a report from Ibn Mas'ud himself: he asked whether God had put him to shame, and Abu Jahl answered that he was nothing more than a man killed by his own people, and then asked which side had the upper hand that day. Told whose day it was, he said only: "You have climbed high, little shepherd." Ibn Ishaq adds that he asked to be struck high, at the neck, so that the head would look well when it was carried.[^s5-13] The head was carried back across the wadi and laid at the Prophet's feet, and he said that it was the Pharaoh of this community.

### Notes

[^s5-1]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the battle of Badr; the wording is close to "if they press close about you, then shoot, and do not draw your swords upon them until they are upon you." Whether this is genuine tactical memory or a later reconstruction deserves a word. It is the kind of detail a pious redactor would not invent: it makes the day begin with the smaller army standing still and shooting rather than charging in faith, and it gives the credit to ground and discipline. It also matches the equipment (see the note on the muster's equipment below): an army with eight swords in it had every reason to keep its distance as long as it could and to make the enemy come through arrow-fire across bad footing. I take the order as substantially historical, while granting that its exact wording has been smoothed. Al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, gives a fuller set of orders in the same sense.
[^s5-2]: al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr; Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids). Mihja' is named as the first of the Muslims killed at Badr in most lists, though a minority of reports give that place to Harith ibn Suraqa; both appear in the casualty list given in the back matter. Al-Waqidi's detail that Harith was hit while drinking at the cistern is the sort of circumstantial touch that makes him valuable and unverifiable in the same breath.
[^s5-3]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the muster for Badr; Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids), where the figures are given as six coats of mail and eight swords among three hundred and thirteen men, with two horses and some seventy camels. The equipment figures are argued in the note to the muster in an earlier section and are not re-argued here; what matters at this point is the consequence, which the sources themselves draw: at Badr the Muslims fought principally with bows, spears and stones. Al-Waqidi has slightly higher counts of mail, which may reflect the equipment taken from the Qurayshi dead being retrospectively read back into the muster.
[^s5-4]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the battle of Badr; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, where the handful is thrown at the moment the general advance is ordered. The Arabic of the cry is *shahat al-wujuh*, "disfigured be the faces." Q 8:17 — "you did not slay them, but God slew them; and you did not throw when you threw, but God threw" — was read by the early exegetes as referring to this act, and al-Tabari, *Tarikh*, the year 2 AH, records that reading along with one that applies the verse to the killing generally. The report is given here as the tradition gives it and is not adjudicated: what can be said in a historian's voice is that a hot wind carrying dust off a dry valley floor into the faces of men advancing westward with the morning sun behind their opponents is a thing that happens at Badr in March, and that the tradition, which had no interest in a naturalistic reading, nonetheless preserved the physical detail of eyes and mouths full of grit.
[^s5-5]: Q 8:9 for the thousand; Q 8:12 for the words quoted, from which this section takes its title. Al 'Imran gives different figures in a passage the exegetes connect with Badr: three thousand at Q 3:124 and five thousand at Q 3:125, the latter conditional on the believers' steadfastness. The three sets of numbers have been harmonised in various ways since the second century — a thousand promised and sent, three thousand promised on condition, five thousand on a further condition; or a thousand at Badr and the larger figures belonging to Uhud, where Q 3:121–129 otherwise sits. I follow the reading that treats Q 8:9 as the figure operative at Badr and the Al 'Imran verses as a fuller promise whose occasion the tradition itself is unsure of, since the Anfal material is the earliest layer and is unambiguously about this day. Ibn Kathir, *al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya*, iii, sets out the harmonisations at length and is candid that the exegetes did not agree.
[^s5-6]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the battle of Badr, for the man of the Banu Mazin ibn al-Najjar; Muslim, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Jihad wa'l-Siyar, for the version from Ibn 'Abbas, in which the Ansari sees the head of the man before him fall as the voice cries out. Hayzum is named in these reports as the horse of Jibril. Bukhari, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Maghazi, bab of the Badr expedition, preserves a report from Ibn 'Abbas of the Prophet's supplication in the shelter and the answer to it, but not the horseman; the horseman comes through the Medinan maghazi tradition and through Muslim.
[^s5-7]: al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, is the fullest collector of these reports; the yellow-turban material, in which the angels came down wearing turbans of the same colour as al-Zubayr's, appears there and is repeated by Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids). The growth is worth watching in sequence. The Qur'anic layer names no angel and describes no combat, only help, a token, and a command. Ibn Ishaq's layer adds Jibril, a named horse, a voice, and men who heard rather than saw. Al-Waqidi and after him the later compilers add numbers, positions in the line, headgear, and named angelic combatants; by the time of the popular sira literature the angels have biographies. That trajectory — from allusion to specification over roughly two centuries — is the ordinary behaviour of this material and is the reason the chapter reports the earliest layer, attributes the middle layer, and mentions the latest only as a growth.
[^s5-8]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the battle of Badr; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, where the blade is named al-'Awn and its descent in 'Ukkasha's family is noted. 'Ukkasha ibn Mihsan was killed fighting Tulayha al-Asadi in the ridda campaigns after the Prophet's death. The survival of the sword as a family heirloom is the mechanism by which such a report reaches the collectors: the object authenticates the story and the story authenticates the object, and the historian can say confidently only that the family of 'Ukkasha possessed a sword they said was this one.
[^s5-9]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the battle of Badr, where the account of the leg and the arm is given on Mu'adh's own authority, including the comparison of the severed leg to a date-stone shot from under the crusher. On who brought Abu Jahl down, the tradition offers three claims and one composite. (i) Bukhari, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Maghazi, bab of the Badr expedition, has the Prophet ask who will find out what became of Abu Jahl, and 'Abdallah ibn Mas'ud find him dying and take his head; Bukhari also carries a report in which two young men of the Ansar — sons of 'Afra, in that version — are named as having killed him, and the Prophet, examining their swords, judges that one of them struck the fatal blow. (ii) Ibn Ishaq gives the sequence followed here: Mu'adh ibn 'Amr ibn al-Jamuh takes the leg, 'Ikrima cuts Mu'adh's arm, Mu'awwidh ibn 'Afra strikes the fallen man and dies later in the fight, and Ibn Mas'ud finishes him. (iii) Al-Waqidi and some of the Ansari family traditions promote Mu'adh ibn 'Afra, brother of Mu'awwidh, to the leg-strike, which looks like a Banu 'Afra claim competing with a Banu Salama one; the two Mu'adhs are easily confused and the confusion is old. I follow Ibn Ishaq because his sequence is the only one that accounts for all the surviving details — Mu'adh ibn al-Jamuh's arm, 'Ikrima's presence, Mu'awwidh's death deep in the enemy line, and Ibn Mas'ud's head — without discarding any of them, and because the competing versions are each traceable to an interested clan while the composite is not. The Prophet's inspection of the two swords, in the Bukhari report, is itself evidence that the question of credit was disputed on the field that afternoon.
[^s5-10]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the battle of Badr, for the whole episode, the shout, and 'Abd al-Rahman's remark about his prisoners and his armour; Bukhari, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Maghazi, bab of the Badr expedition, preserves the account from 'Abd al-Rahman ibn 'Awf, including that the two men had a compact of protection between them from before the hijra. The report is unflattering to Bilal, to the Ansar and to the discipline of the victorious army, and no later hand tidied it; that is a good sign of its age.
[^s5-11]: Al-Baladhuri and the exegetical tradition on Q 58:22 both preserve the report; Ibn Kathir, *al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya*, iii, records it with a note of hesitation, since other reports have al-Jarrah surviving Badr. The doubt is worth registering: the kinship material at Badr attracted illustrative accretion precisely because it was theologically useful, and Abu 'Ubayda's case is the weakest of the set. 'Umar and al-'As ibn Hisham, and Abu Hudhayfa and his father, are on much firmer ground, both being in Ibn Ishaq.
[^s5-12]: The exchange between Abu Bakr and his son 'Abd al-Rahman is preserved in the biographical tradition, Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, iii (the Badr companions), and repeated by Ibn Kathir, *al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya*, iii. It is placed by some at Badr and by others at Uhud; the point of the story is the same in either setting.
[^s5-13]: Bukhari, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Maghazi, bab of the Badr expedition, for the exchange and for "you have climbed high, little shepherd" — the taunt turns on Ibn Mas'ud having herded goats for 'Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt in Mecca; Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the battle of Badr, for the request to strike high at the neck, and for the words over the head, "this was the Pharaoh of this community." Al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, adds that Abu Jahl's regret was to have been killed by a farmer rather than by a man of standing — a variant which, whether or not it is his, is exactly the sentiment the whole battle was about. Whether the Prophet himself fought hand to hand at Badr is a related question, and the answer is probably not: he directed the line from the rise and moved forward with it, and the reports of his throwing dust and pebbles and calling men on are unanimous, but no early source has him exchanging blows, and Ibn Ishaq, who names the killer of nearly every Qurayshi of rank, credits him with none. Al-Waqidi's fuller picture of him in the press is best read as devotional emphasis on presence rather than as a claim about swordplay.
