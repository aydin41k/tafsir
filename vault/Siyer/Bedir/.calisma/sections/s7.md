## What Badr Broke

Abu Sufyan brought the caravan into Mecca and sold it. The goods that had come down from Gaza reached the market they were consigned to, were divided among the shareholders, and turned a profit for a city in mourning. That is the first thing to be said about Badr and the thing most easily lost: the object of the whole expedition, the reason three hundred and thirteen men had walked out of Medina in Ramadan, was obtained by the side that lost the fight. Abu Sufyan's swerve to the coast had worked. He got his cargo home along the beach road while the men who came out to protect him were buried in a dry well eighty miles to the north.[^s7-1]

The column came up to Medina over several days, driving camels it had not owned a week before and herding seventy prisoners who were, many of them, cousins of their guards. Ibn Ishaq reports that the people of the city came out along the road to meet them, the boys running as far as al-Rawha', a long day's march south, so the army was met before it was in sight of the palms. Zayd ibn Haritha had gone ahead with the news; Medina had had two days to draw the conclusion that mattered.[^s7-2]

Something on the order of seventy men of Quraysh had been killed. In a town whose adult men ran to a few thousand, that was a wound, not a mutilation. What made it a catastrophe was not the number. It was which men.

Read the Meccan dead as a list of offices rather than of persons and the shape of the disaster appears at once. 'Utba ibn Rabi'a and his brother Shayba were the senior men of 'Abd Shams, the clan with the largest share in the Syrian trade; 'Utba was the man others were sent to when a quarrel needed settling. Abu Jahl was the effective head of Makhzum, which had run the boycott of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ and his kin, and Makhzum lost a rank of its men with him, as Sahm lost Nubayh and Munabbih, the sons of al-Hajjaj. Umayya ibn Khalaf was chief of Jumah and a guarantor of Meccan credit; Zam'a ibn al-Aswad and al-Harith ibn 'Amir stood in the same rank. Al-Nadr ibn al-Harith of 'Abd al-Dar had carried the banner because his clan carried it by hereditary right, and the right did not survive him in any usable form.[^s7-3]

Mecca had no government. No king, no council with fixed membership, no coercive force, no treasury. What it had instead was a small number of old men whose word bound their clans and whose arrangements — the pacts of protection with the bedouin along the road, the guarantees of safe passage, the sureties for blood-money, the division of shares in a caravan — constituted the whole working order of the town. That kind of authority could not be transferred. It was personal and slow: a man acquired it over decades by judgement, wealth and patronage, and it did not pass to his heir intact. Badr killed the greater part of that generation between sunrise and noon. It did not destroy Mecca's wealth, which was in goods and credit and largely untouched, nor its capacity to raise men, demonstrated at Uhud with three thousand of them. It destroyed Meccan seniority, which is why a fight of some 950 men against three hundred and thirteen mattered out of all proportion to its size.

W. Montgomery Watt read the conflict between Mecca and Medina as an economic one, a struggle over the northern road and the trade that moved along it, and about the road he was substantially right: Mecca's route to Syria ran past a hostile town which could now close it at will. But a road is not only a road. The men who died at Badr were the men who had negotiated the passage of that road, tribe by tribe, over thirty years, and their deaths cost Mecca its arrangements as much as its access.[^s7-4]

Abu Sufyan ibn Harb inherited the leadership of Quraysh by survival. He had not been in the first rank before Badr — he was a caravan master of good family who happened, on 17 Ramadan, to be a hundred miles away with the goods — and he came out of it the senior surviving man of 'Abd Shams and head of the war party, alongside Safwan ibn Umayya, whose father had died at the wells. Both men's standing rested on a killing that had to be answered. That is the mechanism by which Mecca's reply became necessarily a war of revenge rather than a calculation about the road: those who now spoke for the city had fathers, brothers and sons in the pit at Badr. The bereaved of Badr paid for and filled the ranks at Uhud, which follows from the pit as an answer follows a question.[^s7-5]

Inside Medina the change was quieter and more thorough. The Muhajirun — the emigrants from Mecca, who had arrived with nothing and had been living on other men's dates — had gone out and won, and their standing in the town was never afterwards that of dependents. The Ansar, who had undertaken to defend a man and had at Dhafiran committed themselves to attacking his enemies, were past the point where the commitment could be revisited: they had killed the chiefs of Quraysh, they had eight dead of their own, and no arrangement was open to them that did not involve Muhammad's survival. The uncommitted found their position narrowed accordingly, and so did the Jewish tribes of the oasis, whose treaties had been made with a refugee community and now bound them to a victorious one. The Banu Qaynuqa' were expelled in Shawwal of the same year, and that belongs to another chapter.[^s7-6]

Surat al-Anfal, the chapter of the Qur'an that tradition attaches to Badr, is not an account of the battle. Read straight through, it is a document of the aftermath: it opens on the quarrel over the spoils, legislates the fifth — the khums, the portion set aside from captured property before division, dated by the jurists to this day and the first piece of public finance in Islam — rebukes the taking of ransom for the prisoners, and looks back at the day in images rather than narrative: the sleep, the rain, the thousand angels, the handful of dust. What comes nearest to describing the encounter is the verse reminding the believers that they had wanted the other of the two parties, the unarmed one. And it gives the day a name: the day of the furqan, of distinction, the day the two ways were told apart, and by that name the tradition afterwards knew it.[^s7-7]

It also produced a rank. To have been at Badr was a status a man carried for life: he was a badri, one of the people of Badr, and in a community rapidly becoming a state this was the nearest thing to a patent of nobility. 'Umar as caliph paid the badriyyun the highest stipends, and faults were forgiven them that were forgiven to no one else. Lists were kept, and they are why we can name so large a proportion of three hundred and thirteen men — and also why the names need watching, since a rank worth having is a rank worth claiming, and families had a century in which to claim it.[^s7-8]

That caution applies more widely, and is worth stating plainly once. The sources for Badr are good by the standards of seventh-century Arabia and thin by any other: the Qur'anic material is contemporary but allusive, and the narrative accounts were written down a century and more after the event by men who were pious, partisan and dependent on family tradition. Hold the shape of the day with some confidence and the details with less. The numbers are round and convenient. The speeches are reconstructions in the idiom of the reporter, however faithful to the sense. The order of the fighting — who struck whom, and when — is the part of any battle account that survives worst, and at Badr it has been sorted into a series of duels by men writing for audiences who liked duels. What can be trusted is what nobody had an interest in inventing.[^s7-9]

That is where the contingency comes back. A tradition free to shape its foundation story would not have had the believers hoping for the unarmed caravan, or Abu Sufyan getting clean away, or the Meccan column turning for home before a blood-debt shamed it forward, or two clans withdrawing, or the whole collision hanging on there being one reliable water in that stretch of the Hijaz in March. Those details survive because they were remembered. The Prophet lived eight more years; Mecca opened its gates to him six years after Badr, and Abu Sufyan was among the men who came out to arrange the surrender. The fourteen graves on the floor of the wadi are still shown. Everything that followed in Arabia followed from a fight that began because two water-parties met at a well in the dark.

[^s7-3]: The clan affiliations are drawn from Ibn Ishaq's list of the Quraysh dead, clan by clan (Ibn Hisham, *Sira*, the naming of those killed at Badr), checked against Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids). Al-Waqidi's list is shorter in places and differs in a handful of names; the discrepancies fall among the junior dead and do not touch the senior men. Whether Badr can carry the interpretative weight later tradition placed on it is a fair question, and the honest answer is that it can carry this much and not much more: the loss of these particular men is documented in lists compiled independently of the battle narratives, in genealogical and *nasab* material whose interest was in descent rather than in edification. The claim that Mecca's leading generation died at Badr is thus better attested than almost anything in the battle itself. The larger claims — that Badr broke Meccan paganism, or that it was the decisive engagement of Arabian history — are theological verdicts, and the fact that Mecca fought on for six more years and beat the Muslims badly at Uhud is the check on them.

[^s7-4]: W. Montgomery Watt, *Muhammad at Medina*, on the Meccan trade and the strategy of the raids; the argument runs through his account of the years 1–3 AH. Watt's economic reading has been criticised on two grounds. Fred M. Donner, "Mecca's Food Supplies and Muhammad's Boycott," argues that the traffic at issue was less grand and more local than the Watt picture implies — grain and food as much as luxury transit — which if anything strengthens the case that interference with it was intolerable. The other objection is that Watt's frame leaves too little room for what the participants said they were doing; the sources are unembarrassed about religion as a cause, and a reading that treats the religious language as a covering for the commercial one is making a choice the evidence does not compel. The position taken here is that the road was the occasion of the war and the seniority of Quraysh was the casualty of it.

The identification of *al-furqan* in 8:41 with the day of Badr is the standard exegetical reading and is as old as the earliest commentaries, but it is a reading and not a statement: the verse says "the day of the *furqan*, the day the two hosts met," and the gloss depends on taking the second clause as defining the first, which is natural but not inevitable, since *furqan* elsewhere in the Qur'an denotes revelation itself rather than an event. The chapter follows the traditional identification because the immediate context is manifestly the division of spoils after a battle. On the *khums* as dated to Badr, see the fiscal chapters of the legal literature and al-Baladhuri on the early division of spoils; the jurists are unanimous that the rule of the fifth begins here.

On the lists: the surviving rosters of the three hundred and thirteen were assembled well over a century after the day, and they are longer than they should be. Al-Waqidi and Ibn Sa'd both name men whose presence other authorities deny, and the direction of error is predictable, since descent from a *badri* was worth money and standing under the Umayyads and worth more under the 'Abbasids. The names of the dead are the most secure part of the record; the names of the survivors are the least.

[^s7-9]: On the sources generally, see "A Note on the Sources" below. The specific point about duels is worth isolating: al-Waqidi's Badr is organised to a striking degree around single combats with named participants on both sides, and the *maghazi* — the literature of the raids — had by his time acquired an audience for exactly that. Ibn Ishaq's account, a generation earlier, is looser and less complete, and where the two differ the direction of growth is almost always from vagueness towards circumstantial detail rather than the reverse. This is the single most useful rule of thumb a reader of Badr can carry.
