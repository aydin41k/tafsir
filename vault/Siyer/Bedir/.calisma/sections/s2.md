## Three Hundred and Thirteen

Damdam ibn 'Amr al-Ghifari came into the valley of Mecca on a camel whose ears he had slit and whose nose he had bloodied, standing in his saddle, his shirt turned back to front. Abu Sufyan had hired him on the road north and sent him down the coast at speed, three hundred miles and more, to say one thing in the one form nobody in the city could mistake. The mutilated beast, the reversed saddle, the torn and inverted garment: each was a recognised sign of catastrophe, and together they meant that a man had ridden past the point of caring for his property or his dignity. Ibn Ishaq has him cry it out in front of the Ka'ba: "O Quraysh, the merchandise! Abu Sufyan is with it, and Muhammad and his companions have cut him off. I do not think you will reach it. Help! Help!"[^s2-1]

Three nights earlier, by the account Ibn Ishaq gives in his Sirat Rasul Allah, 'Atika bint 'Abd al-Muttalib had woken frightened and sent for her brother al-'Abbas. She had seen a rider come into the valley on a camel, she said, and halt, and cry three times: "Set out, O treacherous people, to your deaths in three days." The people gathered to him. Then he was on the roof of the Ka'ba crying the same words, and then on the shoulder of Abu Qubays, the mountain over the town, crying them a third time; and then he took up a boulder and loosed it down the slope, and it broke as it came, and there was not a house or a dwelling in Mecca that did not have a fragment of it inside. Al-'Abbas repeated the dream and it went round the town. Abu Jahl heard it and jeered: another prophetess among the sons of 'Abd al-Muttalib, and if nothing had happened in three days they would be written down as the greatest liars in the sanctuary. On the third day Damdam rode in. Al-'Abbas found Abu Jahl at the Ka'ba as the clans were arming, and said, "This is what she saw."[^s2-2]

Mecca was on the road inside two days. There was nothing improvised about the shape of the muster and everything improvised about its purpose: each clan of Quraysh sent its men behind its own banner, and a man who would not go was expected to pay for a substitute, so that staying at home cost either money or standing.[^s2-3] Old debts were suspended and old feuds set aside to get the column out. Nine or ten of the great men undertook to feed the whole army by turns, each taking a day and slaughtering camels for it — an ostentation that would later tell the Muslims almost exactly how many mouths were in the valley. The column went out with drums, with singing-women in it to abuse the enemy in verse, with a hundred horses led at the front and flanks, and with mail and shields in quantity: al-Waqidi, whose figures for equipment run into the hundreds, is describing a force that could put an armoured man into the line wherever it chose.[^s2-4]

Two absences are worth as much as the muster. Abu Lahab, uncle to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ and one of the loudest of his enemies, did not go. He had a claim of four thousand dirhams against al-'As ibn Hisham ibn al-Mughira, who was bankrupt, and he sent al-'As out in his place in settlement of the debt — a man's body against a sum of silver, which is the plainest statement anywhere in the sources of what the levy was worth in cash.[^s2-5] The whole clan of Banu 'Adi ibn Ka'b, 'Umar ibn al-Khattab's people, sent nobody at all, and were neither shamed into it nor apparently much pressed; no blood of 'Adi was shed on either side at Badr.[^s2-6]

Where shame was applied it worked. Umayya ibn Khalaf of Banu Jumah, heavy and elderly and among the first men of Mecca, had decided to stay. 'Uqba ibn Abi Mu'ayt came to him where he sat among his people carrying a censer of burning incense and a kohl-stick, set them down in front of him, and told him to perfume himself and paint his eyes, for he was one of the women. It was the most direct insult available in that society, and it was delivered in public, in front of the men whose regard was the whole of Umayya's position. He answered, "God curse you and what you have brought," bought the fastest camel in Mecca, armed himself, and marched.[^s2-7]

The other column had gone out first, and it had gone out smaller. The Prophet left Medina on 12 Ramadan with three hundred and thirteen men — the number the tradition has kept and repeated more consistently than any other figure in the day, and the one I follow — of whom something over eighty were Muhajirun, the emigrants from Mecca, and the rest Ansar of Aws and Khazraj.[^s2-8] They had two horses. Al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam rode one and al-Miqdad ibn 'Amr the other, and there was no third; against the hundred coming up from Mecca this was not cavalry but two mounted messengers. They had about seventy camels for three hundred and thirteen men, which meant three and sometimes four to a beast, each riding his stage and then walking while the others rode, in the last third of a fasting month, on a road going south-west into rising heat. There were six coats of mail in the army and eight swords.[^s2-9] Everyone else carried a bow, a spear, a staff, or stones.

The Prophet took his place in the rotation. He shared a camel with 'Ali ibn Abi Talib and Marthad ibn Abi Marthad al-Ghanawi, and when they offered him the whole ride he refused it: he was no stronger than they, he said, and no less in need of the reward.[^s2-10] Mus'ab ibn 'Umayr carried the white standard at the head of the column, 'Ali the black banner of the Muhajirun, Sa'd ibn Mu'adh the banner of the Ansar. Ibn Umm Maktum, who was blind, was left to lead the prayer in the city; at al-Rawha', a day out, Abu Lubaba ibn 'Abd al-Mundhir was turned back to hold Medina itself. Boys who had attached themselves to the column were sent home. It was an expedition of the ordinary kind, aimed at a caravan of about a thousand camels coming down the coast, and it was not equipped for anything else.

The news reached them in the valley of Dhafiran, four days out and perhaps twenty miles short of Badr: that Quraysh had marched from Mecca in force to defend the caravan. What followed there is the most consequential half-hour of the campaign, and it happened in talk. Ibn Ishaq has the Prophet lay the changed position before the men and ask them plainly for their counsel — "Advise me, O people" — which is not the act of a commander who considered the matter settled, and not the shape a later writer would have given the scene if he were free to invent it.[^s2-11] Abu Bakr spoke, and spoke for going on. 'Umar spoke to the same effect. Then al-Miqdad ibn 'Amr stood up for the emigrants and said that they would not say what the Children of Israel said to Moses — go you and your Lord and fight — but rather, go, and we fight with you; if he led them to Bark al-Ghimad, at the far end of the world as they reckoned it, they would go with him to it.

And the Prophet asked again for counsel.

He was waiting for the Ansar, and everybody present understood why. The men of Aws and Khazraj had pledged themselves at 'Aqaba to protect him in their own town, as they protected their own children — a compact of sanctuary, not of campaign. Nothing in it obliged them to march four days into the desert and stand against the assembled clans of Quraysh, and two-thirds of the column were Ansar. Sa'd ibn Mu'adh, chief of Aws, saw what the repeated question meant and said so: it seemed the Prophet meant them. He was told yes. Ibn Ishaq's report of what Sa'd answered has the cadence of something remembered rather than composed: they had believed him and borne witness that what he brought was true and given their oaths to obey; let him go where he wished and they were with him; and by Him who sent him, if he went down into the sea they would go into it with him, and not one of them would stay behind.[^s2-12] It was consent given by men who could have refused on the letter of an agreement, and it converted a raiding party into an army by the only means available.

What it did not do was decide who they would meet. The Qur'an is unusually direct about this, and it is the earliest layer of evidence there is: Surat al-Anfal, addressing the believers afterwards, recalls that God had promised them one of the two parties and that they had wanted the other of the two — the unarmed one, the one with the camels and the cloth on it — while God intended to establish the truth and cut off the unbelievers.[^s2-13] The passage is a rebuke of a preference, and a rebuke only makes sense if the preference was real. Three hundred and thirteen men had come out for a caravan. They had just agreed to go on if the alternative was forced on them; they had not agreed to seek it, and nobody at Dhafiran proposed marching on the Meccan army where it stood. They went forward towards the water.

Two columns were now on the road, and each of them was still looking for something other than the other.

[^s2-1]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the muster of Quraysh; and see al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, who adds that Damdam was hired for the errand and gives the wording of the cry with small variations. The mutilation of the messenger's beast and the reversal of saddle and shirt are described by the sources as a recognised signal rather than explained, which suggests the practice was still legible to their first audiences; al-Waqidi alone specifies the bloodied nose. Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids), compresses the whole episode into a sentence.
[^s2-2]: Ibn Ishaq, in Guillaume, *The Life of Muhammad*, 290–291 (the dream of 'Atika and Abu Jahl's reply). The report comes through Hashimite channels — it is 'Atika's dream, told to al-'Abbas, and the punchline is al-'Abbas scoring off Abu Jahl in public — and its shape is suspiciously perfect: the dream-rider does precisely what Damdam then did, three days later, in the same valley. Family tradition of the 'Abbasid period had every reason to preserve and polish a story in which the Prophet's own house saw the disaster coming and the leader of Makhzum sneered at the warning. Against dismissing it altogether: the tradition also records a closely parallel dream by a Meccan of no such lineage, Juhaym ibn al-Salt, at a halt on the march, with Abu Jahl making almost the same joke — which suggests that dream-omens circulating in the Meccan column before Badr are a genuine memory even if the individual dreams have been worked up. This account narrates 'Atika's dream as Ibn Ishaq narrates it and takes no view on what she saw.
[^s2-3]: al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, is the fullest on the mechanics of the levy: the clan quotas, the substitution rule, the suspension of debts. Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the march of Quraysh, gives the clan contingents without the administrative detail.
[^s2-4]: al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, for the drums, the singing-women, the hundred horses and the six hundred to seven hundred camels; the feeding rota of nine or ten camels a day, which becomes the count of the army in the next section, is in both Ibn Ishaq and al-Waqidi. Al-Waqidi's Meccan equipment figures (commonly given as six or seven hundred coats of mail) are of the same suspiciously symmetrical kind as his six on the Muslim side, and are best read as a statement of proportion rather than an inventory.
[^s2-5]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the muster of Quraysh, for Abu Lahab's substitute and the four thousand dirhams; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, adds that al-'As's debt was owed for gambling. Ibn Hisham preserves the tradition that Abu Lahab also relied on the dream-omens as a pretext. The figure 4,000 dirhams is the same sum that recurs as a standard ransom for a prisoner after the battle, which may mean it was simply the going rate for a man of standing, or may mean that one round number has attracted the other.
[^s2-6]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the march of Quraysh, on Banu 'Adi ibn Ka'b; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, is more explicit that the clan stayed home as a body. The absence is a genuine datum and not a courtesy to 'Umar's people: the sources are equally frank that Banu 'Adi produced no martyr and no prisoner on either side.
[^s2-7]: Ibn Ishaq, in Guillaume, *The Life of Muhammad*, 292 (Umayya ibn Khalaf, the censer and the kohl-stick). The insult works by assigning a man the two visible marks of a woman's toilet, incense and eye-paint, in front of his clan; al-Waqidi has the same scene with 'Uqba saying the words in the assembly rather than setting the objects down. Umayya's own reason for staying is given variously as age, weight, and specific fear of the Medinan side, he having been Bilal's owner and tormentor in Mecca.
[^s2-8]: The count. Three hundred and thirteen is the figure of Ibn Ishaq, al-Waqidi and Ibn Sa'd, and it is what Bukhari's reports of the men of Badr assume; the variants are 314, 317 and 319 in scattered reports, and a minority 307. Some of the spread is bookkeeping — whether to count Abu Lubaba, sent back from al-Rawha' to hold Medina but given a share of the spoils, and Talha ibn 'Ubaydallah and Sa'id ibn Zayd, who were away on the northern road and reached Medina after the army had gone, all three of whom were reckoned *badri* afterwards. The internal breakdown most often given, and the only one that sums without a remainder, is 82 or 83 Muhajirun, 61 of Aws and 170 of Khazraj. That the number matches the three hundred and thirteen who crossed the river with Saul in the Islamic retelling of the story of Gideon's band was noticed early and has been used to argue that the figure is a topos; but 313 is an awkward number to invent, the badri lists are long and circumstantial, and the parallel was drawn by later commentators rather than by the earliest reporters. See Bukhari, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Maghazi, bab of the number of the men of Badr.
[^s2-9]: The equipment. Two horses is the general report, with al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam and al-Miqdad ibn 'Amr named as the riders; a minority of reports make it three and give the third to Marthad ibn Abi Marthad. Seventy camels, sometimes seventy-odd, is stable across the sources. Six coats of mail and eight swords come from al-Waqidi, and they are the kind of figure a historian should hold at arm's length: they are exactly small, exactly memorable, and they serve a point the tradition wanted made. What supports them is the substance rather than the arithmetic — the Muslim army fought largely with bows, spears, stones and clubs, and the sources record particular weapons as individually notable in a way that would be absurd if there had been two hundred swords in the ranks. This account takes six and eight as round and rhetorical and the poverty they describe as real. al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr; Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids).
[^s2-10]: al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, for the camel-rotation and the Prophet's refusal of the whole ride; Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids), names 'Ali ibn Abi Talib and Marthad ibn Abi Marthad as his partners, with Zayd ibn Haritha in some versions. Ibn Ishaq gives the three banners and the arrangements for Medina: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the departure for Badr.
[^s2-11]: Ibn Ishaq, in Guillaume, *The Life of Muhammad*, 294 (the consultation and al-Miqdad's speech); Muslim, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Jihad wa'l-Siyar, preserves a version of the consultation from Anas ibn Malik in which al-Miqdad is the speaker for the Muhajirun and Sa'd for the Ansar. Whether the scene is one occasion or a composite is a fair question: the sources place the consultation at Dhafiran but the final commitment nearer the water, and the two lie about ten miles apart on the same stage of the march, so the accounts may have gathered into one meeting a discussion that ran over a day. The Qur'anic allusion at Q 8:5–6 to believers disputing after the matter was made clear to them implies argument, not a single tidy assent, which is a reason for thinking the debate real and its arrangement into set speeches later. al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, adds speakers and lengthens the speeches, in his usual manner.
[^s2-12]: Ibn Ishaq, in Guillaume, *The Life of Muhammad*, 294 (Sa'd ibn Mu'adh's speech). A substantial minority tradition, followed by al-Waqidi and by Ibn Sa'd in one place, gives the speech to Sa'd ibn 'Ubada, chief of Khazraj. Sa'd ibn Mu'adh is to be preferred: he carried the Ansar's banner at Badr, he appears repeatedly in the battle narrative (the guard on the shelter, the attempt to save Umayya ibn Khalaf), and Sa'd ibn 'Ubada is absent from the badri lists altogether — the reports that he was excused for illness or for a snakebite look like an attempt to account for the absence of a man whose eminence made his absence surprising. The likeliest explanation of the doublet is the shared name and the later prominence of Khazraj in Medina. The "if you went into the sea" clause appears in both versions and in a third, attributed to al-Miqdad, which suggests it was a formula of assent rather than one man's coinage. The pledge at 'Aqaba and its territorial limits: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the second 'Aqaba; and see Watt, *Muhammad at Medina*, ch. 1, on the pact's terms.
[^s2-13]: Q 8:5–7. The sequence — "as He brought you out of your house in truth, while a party of the believers were averse; disputing with you about the truth after it was made clear, as though they were being driven to death while they looked on; and when God promised you one of the two parties, that it should be yours, and you wished that it were the one without strength" — is the earliest evidence for the object of the expedition and the hardest to explain away, since it concedes reluctance and a preference for plunder over battle. Later exegesis softens "the one without strength" by making the preference prudential rather than acquisitive; the plain sense is the caravan. Ibn Kathir, *al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya*, iii, assembles the reports on the occasion of the verses.
