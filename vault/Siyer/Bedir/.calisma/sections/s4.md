## The Night of Rain

It rained in the night. It was the middle of March, the tail of the season when squalls come off
the Red Sea and cross the coastal plain in an hour, and the rain fell unevenly across the valley:
hard on the western side, where Quraysh were camped under the sandhill, and light on the eastern
side, where the Muslims lay. What that did to the ground had already been decided by the ground
itself — the loose red sand at the foot of al-'Aqanqal took the water and held it, and the flatter,
gravelled floor on the Medinan side set under it like a floor.[^s4-1] Men on one bank woke to mud
that pulled at the foot. Men on the other woke to a surface they could run on.

The Muslims slept. Ibn Ishaq, whose account of the expedition comes down to us in Ibn Hisham's
edition of the *Sirat Rasul Allah*, reports that a deep and easy drowsiness came over the camp that
night, and that men who had every reason to lie awake — three hundred of them, eighty miles from
home, with something on the order of nine hundred and fifty of their own tribesmen and neighbours
across the wadi — lay down beside their weapons and slept soundly until the call to prayer. Surat
al-Anfal names the sleep and tells the believers what it was: a security sent down upon them from
God, and water sent down from the sky to purify them, to take away the whisperings of Satan and to
make firm their feet.[^s4-2] The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ did not sleep.

Sa'd ibn Mu'adh had seen to the shelter before dark. It was an 'arish — a rough booth of palm
branches, the kind a man makes for shade at a well — and the working party of Ansar raised it on a
low rise a hundred and fifty paces or so behind the centre of the line, high enough that a man
standing in its mouth could see the whole frontage and the pass beyond it. Sa'd posted a guard of
his own people around it. He also had riding camels kept saddled and kneeling beside it, and Ibn
Ishaq has him say why: if God gave them the victory, that was what they wanted; and if the day went
the other way, the Prophet would reach his own people in Medina, and behind him there were men who
loved him no less than the three hundred who had come out.[^s4-3] It was not a comfortable thing to
say the night before a battle, and its survival in the tradition is the best argument for its being
remembered rather than composed.

He prayed most of the night standing by the trunk of a tree beside the shelter, and towards the end
of it he raised both hands towards the qibla and did not lower them. The wording of what he said
varies from report to report; the shortest and earliest form of it, which Bukhari preserves from Ibn
'Abbas, is the one to hold: "O God, fulfil for me what You have promised me. O God, if this band
perishes this day, You will not be worshipped on the earth."[^s4-4] He pressed it so long that the
cloak slipped from his shoulders and lay on the ground behind him. Abu Bakr was in the mouth of the
shelter with a drawn sword. He came in, picked up the cloak, put it back across the Prophet's
shoulders, took him by the hand, and told him that this was enough — that he had pressed his Lord
long enough, and that God would give him what He had promised.

The night of 16–17 Ramadan 2 AH ended at about a quarter past five. The seventeenth is the date this
chapter follows, and it fell on a Friday.[^s4-5] Bilal called the prayer from the front of the camp
and the army formed up in ranks to pray, armed, in the order in which it was going to fight. The
tradition is divided about whether they fasted that day; most probably they did, no leave to break
the fast on the march having yet been given.[^s4-6] Then the Prophet walked the length of the line — a front of about a hundred and twenty paces, which is what
three hundred men in three ranks come to — and told them to be patient, not to break the ranks and
not to begin anything before the word was given, and that no man of them would meet the enemy that
day and be killed pressing forward, not turning back, but God would bring him into the Garden.

He dressed the line himself, with an arrow-shaft in his hand, using it the way a man uses a rod to
sight along a row and push a shoulder back into it. Al-Waqidi, whose *Kitab al-Maghazi* is the
fullest source for the ground and the small business of that morning, has him press the shaft into
the belly of Sawad ibn Ghaziyya, who was standing out in front of his place in the rank. It hurt.
Sawad said so: God had sent him with truth and justice, and he wanted his redress. The Prophet
uncovered his own belly and told him to take it. Sawad bent, put his arms round him and kissed the
place, and when he was asked what he had meant by it said that he had wanted his last act in this
world to be that.[^s4-7] Before he went back to the shelter he walked out into the empty ground
between the armies and marked with the shaft the patches of sand on which particular men of Quraysh
would fall, naming them: 'Utba, Shayba, al-Walid, Abu Jahl, Umayya ibn Khalaf, al-Nadr ibn
al-Harith.[^s4-8]

Three hundred and thirteen men stood in line in a fasting month with eight swords among them.

Quraysh came over the sandhill between about half past six and seven. They had to: the pass at the
foot of al-'Aqanqal was the only practicable way down onto the valley floor, and the whole army with
its hundred horses and its seven hundred camels had to funnel through it and then spread. The image
the sources reach for is of sand-hills on the move, a broad dark mass sliding down a long slope, and
what the reports also preserve is that the slope was working against them from the first step: the
rain-soaked sand slid and gave under hoof and heel, and men came down onto the flat already blown.
They deployed on a frontage rather wider than the Muslim one, overlapping both flanks, cavalry
divided between the wings, al-Nadr ibn al-Harith carrying the standard in the centre, the baggage and
the camels parked behind them at the dune's foot. The sun, coming up over the eastern shoulder of the
valley, was in their faces.

From the mouth of the shelter the Prophet watched them form up and raised his hands again. Ibn Ishaq
gives the words: here were Quraysh, come in their vanity and their pride, calling His Messenger a
liar; and he asked for the help he had been promised, and asked that they be brought low that
morning.[^s4-9] Down the Muslim line the watchword passed from man to man, one word repeated —
*ahad*, one — and the line did not move.

The first man across the gap went for the water. Al-Aswad ibn 'Abd al-Asad al-Makhzumi was a
quarrelsome man with a reputation for it, and he came out of the Qurayshi right alone, running, on an
oath that he would drink from the Muslims' cistern or destroy it or die in front of it. Hamza ibn
'Abd al-Muttalib stepped out of the Muslim right to meet him at the cistern's edge and took his leg
off below the knee with one stroke. Al-Aswad fell, and then dragged himself forward on his hands into
the basin to get at the water and keep the first clause of his oath, and Hamza came down after him
and killed him in it.[^s4-10] The whole thing occupied a minute and happened off the axis of the
battle, at the edge of the field, over a cistern.

Then the formal part began. An Arab battle opened, where both sides consented to it, with *mubaraza*
— single combats fought out in the gap between the lines, champion against champion, while the armies
stood and watched — and out of the Qurayshi right came three men of one house: 'Utba ibn Rabi'a, his
brother Shayba ibn Rabi'a, and 'Utba's son al-Walid ibn 'Utba. They stood in the open and called for
men of their own standing. Three young men of the Ansar answered first: 'Awf and Mu'awwidh, the sons
of 'Afra, and 'Abdallah ibn Rawaha. They named themselves, and were told to go back — the Quraysh had
no business with them and wanted their equals, from their own kin. Ibn Ishaq's sequence, which I
follow, has the herald of Quraysh then call across to the Muslim line for peers out of the tribe, and
the Prophet send the three Ansar back to their exact places in the rank.[^s4-11]

He called three men of his own house out instead, by name: stand up, 'Ubayda ibn al-Harith; stand up,
Hamza; stand up, 'Ali. They walked out and named their fathers, and the Quraysh acknowledged them as
worthy. 'Ubayda, the eldest and heaviest of the three, took 'Utba; Hamza took Shayba; 'Ali, the
youngest man in the gap, took al-Walid. Two of the three pairs were finished almost at once. Hamza
cut Shayba down and Shayba did not get up. 'Ali gave al-Walid no time at all. The third pair, the two
old men, fought on and wounded each other, and then 'Utba's sword took 'Ubayda's leg from under him
and he went down where he stood. Hamza and 'Ali turned from their own dead and closed on 'Utba
together and killed him over his body.

They carried 'Ubayda back on a cloak, ninety paces to the line and up the rise to the shelter, and
the Prophet laid his head on his own foot. Ibn Ishaq has him say, of the poetry Abu Talib had once
made in defence of the family, that if his uncle could see him now he would know that he himself had
the better right to have said it. The marrow showed in the stump. He died of the wound days later at
al-Safra' on the road home and was buried there.[^s4-12] Of the three men who had walked out of the
Qurayshi right, all three were dead inside half an hour, and they were a father, an uncle and a son
of one of the two or three first houses of Mecca. A ripple went along the Qurayshi right that
everyone in both armies could see.

Then the lines closed, and after that nobody could see the whole of it.

### Notes

[^s4-1]: The rain is in all the main accounts of the night: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the night before Badr; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, who is the one who insists on its unequal effect on the two banks. The question is whether the differential is a genuine memory or an inference read backwards out of Q 8:11 ("and He sent down upon you water from the sky, to purify you … and to make firm thereby your feet"), whose last clause invites exactly this gloss. Two things argue for a real squall. The first is the date: mid-March is the end of the Hijaz's brief wet season, and a night squall at Badr then is unremarkable rather than providential-looking. The second is that the effect the sources describe follows from the geology of the site independently of anyone's theology — the western bank at Badr is loose aeolian sand under the flank of al-'Aqanqal, which slumps when wet, and the eastern floor is a gravel-and-silt wash which compacts. A storyteller inventing a miracle would not have arrived at so mechanical a benefit, and would probably have made the rain fall on one side only. The verse rests on the squall; the squall was not generated by the verse. The position is close to al-Mubarakpuri's, and does not require his confidence about quantities.

[^s4-2]: Q 8:11. The sleep also appears at Q 3:154 in connection with Uhud, which is worth noticing: it is a recurring Qur'anic figure for a composure given from outside a man, not a detail generated for Badr. Ibn Ishaq narrates the drowsiness without the verse attached; the pairing is standard in the commentators. For the report itself, Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the night before Badr; and see also al-Tabari, *Tarikh*, the year 2 AH.

[^s4-3]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the building of the 'arish; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, has the fullest version, including the saddled camels. Some later writers, uneasy at a proposal that contemplates defeat and flight, either drop the camels or transpose Sa'd's words into a pure declaration of loyalty. The uneasiness is itself evidence: material of this kind is not invented in a tradition that wants the day foreordained, and a later hand smoothing the record removes it rather than adding it. Whether the shelter had any other occupant than Abu Bakr is a minor question — al-Waqidi admits others to it at moments, every account keeps Abu Bakr there throughout.

[^s4-4]: Bukhari, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Maghazi, bab of the Badr expedition; also Muslim, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Jihad wa'l-Siyar, in the report of 'Umar ibn al-Khattab, which adds "O God, grant me what You have promised me" and, on the Prophet's coming out of the shelter, the words of Q 54:45 ("the host shall be routed"). Ibn Ishaq's version in the *Sira* is longer and more rhetorical, and al-Waqidi's longer still, with the appeal against Qurayshi pride and the request for the promised help fused into the night vigil rather than kept separate from it (see note 9 below). The two-clause form in the two Sahih collections is the earliest layer and the one quoted here; the expansions are of the ordinary kind, in which a remembered petition acquires the cadence of liturgy.

[^s4-5]: 17 Ramadan 2 AH is the majority date and the one used by Ibn Ishaq, al-Waqidi and Ibn Sa'd; al-Tabari records it and also preserves the variants of the nineteenth and the twenty-first, and a few reports put the battle on the twelfth. See Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids); al-Tabari, *Tarikh*, the year 2 AH. The conversion of 17 Ramadan 2 AH to 13 March 624 depends on the tabular Hijri calendar and on where one places the epoch, and is good to within a day or two rather than exactly; the astronomical Friday for that date is one reason the seventeenth is preferable to its rivals, since the Friday is independently and repeatedly attested. Readers should hold the day of the month more loosely than the season, which is secure: everything in the accounts — the rain, the state of the wells, the caravan's timing — belongs to early spring.

[^s4-6]: Ibn Ishaq has the army fasting; a group of reports in al-Waqidi and in the hadith collections on the traveller's fast has the Prophet ordering the fast broken before contact, and the Companions' later disagreement about fasting on campaign is often argued from Badr in both directions. Bukhari, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Sawm, has material for the licence; Muslim, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Siyam, likewise. The licence-reports mostly concern the march to Mecca in 8 AH, and their attachment to Badr looks like backward extension. Since the contact came in the first hour after sunrise, the practical difference is small in any case: fasting or not, these men had eaten nothing since the previous sunset and had marched and dug for two days before that.

[^s4-7]: al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr; Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids), in the notice of Sawad ibn Ghaziyya. The name is given as Sawad ibn Ghaziyya of the Banu 'Adi ibn al-Najjar; some manuscripts have Suwayd. The story is of a type — the reciprocal-justice anecdote — and that makes it suspect as an anecdote while leaving the arrow-shaft, which is corroborated independently as the instrument with which the ranks were dressed, entirely secure.

[^s4-8]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the morning of Badr; al-Waqidi is fuller and gives more names. The marking of the fall-places is one of the elements the tradition treats as foreknowledge, and it is reported here as the tradition reports it. What can be said in the historian's own voice is narrower and still interesting: that men of the Muslim army afterwards remembered particular deaths as having happened on particular patches of a valley floor they had spent a day and a night looking at, and that the memory took this shape.

[^s4-9]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the morning of Badr. The second supplication, made as the Qurayshi line formed up, is distinct in Ibn Ishaq from the night vigil; al-Waqidi runs them together, and Ibn Kathir, *al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya*, iii, sets the versions side by side without choosing. I keep them separate because the two petitions do different work — one asks for the promise, the other names the men across the wadi — and because a fused version is the easier direction of travel.

[^s4-10]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the fighting at Badr; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, who gives the oath in direct speech and the detail of the crawl into the basin. Al-Aswad ibn 'Abd al-Asad was of Makhzum, Abu Jahl's clan. That the first death of the day was over drinking water, on the edge of the field and outside the formal opening of the battle, is the kind of detail a shaped narrative loses rather than gains.

[^s4-11]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the single combats; and see Ibn Kathir, *al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya*, iii. A second family of reports has the Rabi'a brothers challenge and the three Hashimites answer directly, with no Ansari intervention at all, and it is easy to see why that version might be preferred by a later transmitter: it is tidier, and it spares the Ansar a refusal. The Ansari-first sequence is preferable precisely because it costs something. It also explains the shape of the Qurayshi demand, which is not merely for champions but for peers — a demand about rank and lineage, made by men who would not be killed by farmers of Yathrib, and which only makes sense as an answer to somebody. Al-Waqidi keeps the three Ansar and names them as here; the identification of the two sons of 'Afra as 'Awf and Mu'awwidh is his, and Mu'awwidh reappears later in the day at Abu Jahl's side.

[^s4-12]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the single combats, for the words about Abu Talib and the Prophet's laying 'Ubayda's head on his foot; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, for the severed leg and the marrow. On where he died the reports differ between al-Safra' on the return road, which Ibn Ishaq implies and which most later writers follow, and Medina some days after the army got back; al-Safra' is the better attested and is followed here. 'Ubayda ibn al-Harith ibn al-Muttalib was a first cousin once removed of the Prophet and something over sixty years old, which makes the pairing against 'Utba a matter of matching age and standing rather than of matching strength.
