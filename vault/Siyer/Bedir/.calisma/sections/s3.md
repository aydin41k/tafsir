## The Valley of Yalyal

The plain of Badr lay about eighty miles south-west of Medina, where the caravan road from Syria bent inland from the sea. It was not a valley in the sense a reader in a wet country would understand, but a wadi — a watercourse that carries water only after rain and is dry sand the rest of the year — and the wadi at Badr was called Yalyal. It gathered the run-off of the highlands behind Medina and swung away west-north-west toward the Red Sea, dying in the coastal sands short of the shore. Through the battlefield it ran not in one channel but in three or four braided ones, a few paces wide and knee-deep: nothing that would stop a man, but enough that a rank crossing one came out of it untidy.[^s3-1]

The floor at Badr was an open bowl, perhaps two miles long and a mile across where the ground was flat enough to stand an army on, closed by dark rock on the north and east, by broken rocky ground on the south, and on the west by a single great wall of sand. There was no water on the surface. What made Badr worth a name was that the water lay in the sand four or five fathoms down, shallow enough to reach with a hand-dug shaft and a leather bucket, and men had dug there: seven or eight wells strung along the north-eastern edge of the floor, with date-palms and walled plots behind them. In the middle of March, at the tail of the winter rains, there was no other reliable water within a day's march in any direction, and both armies knew it before either arrived.[^s3-2]

The wall of sand on the western side was al-'Aqanqal, and it decided the day. It ran for over a mile, its crest sixty to ninety feet above the floor, and it was a dune and not a rock ridge with sand on it: the back slope, on the Mecca side, was gentle enough to walk a column up; the inner face was a slip-face of loose red sand standing at thirty degrees. Beneath it lay a belt of low dunes and hollows a third of a mile deep, the only ground on which a force coming up the Mecca road could form. The eastern side, where the wells were, was different in kind — packed sand over gravel, firm in any weather, with a gravel gap between the hills through which the Medina track came down. The Qur'an later fixed the geometry in two phrases, the nearer bank and the farther bank, the nearer being nearer to Medina.[^s3-3] Sight-lines ran a mile, so whichever army arrived second would be seen arriving.

The Prophet Muhammad ﷺ had sent two men of Juhayna ahead to the water days before, Basbas ibn 'Amr and 'Adi ibn Abi'l-Zaghba', local men who could sit at a Badr well without being strangers, and they had brought back the caravan's timing from two women quarrelling over a debt. Now, on the night of the sixteenth of Ramadan, he sent 'Ali ibn Abi Talib, al-Zubayr ibn al-'Awwam and Sa'd ibn Abi Waqqas down to the wells to lie up and wait. They took two men who came for water, slaves of the Meccan army sent with skins, one named Aslam.[^s3-4]

The first questioning went badly, and the sources preserve it going badly. The Muslims, still hoping for a caravan, asked where Abu Sufyan was; the two said they were water-carriers of Quraysh; the Muslims disbelieved them and beat them until they said what was wanted, which was that they were Abu Sufyan's men. Ibn Ishaq has the Prophet come over from his prayer and tell his own men that they had beaten the two for telling the truth and let them alone for lying.[^s3-5] Then he put his own questions. Where were Quraysh? Behind that hill, at al-'Aqanqal. How many? Many. How many exactly? They did not know. How many camels did they slaughter in a day? One day nine, the next day ten.

That was the count, because a slaughtered camel fed roughly a hundred men for a day, and any Arab who had provisioned a raiding party knew the conversion. Ibn Ishaq has the Prophet say it aloud: they were between nine hundred and a thousand men. It is a figure that arrives with its working shown, which is why this account prefers it, at roughly nine hundred and fifty, to the round thousand of the later tradition.[^s3-6] He asked next which of the great men were with them, and the two named them off: 'Utba ibn Rabi'a and his brother Shayba, Abu Jahl, Umayya ibn Khalaf, Suhayl ibn 'Amr, al-Nadr ibn al-Harith, Zam'a ibn al-Aswad, Nubayh and Munabbih the sons of al-Hajjaj, Hakim ibn Hizam. The Prophet turned to his companions and said that Mecca had thrown to them the choicest pieces of her own liver.

Against those nine hundred and fifty stood three hundred and thirteen. Quraysh had a hundred horses and seven hundred camels; the Muslims two horses and about seventy camels ridden in turns. Quraysh had also brought drums, wine, and singing-women to lampoon the Muslims by name — the furniture of a display rather than of a campaign, and a fair indication of what the men who organised the march expected to have to do at the end of it.[^s3-7]

What they had come out to do was already done, and done without them. Abu Sufyan had turned his caravan hard west onto the dry coastal strip and run it south through the night without a watering halt, twenty or thirty miles seaward of the wadi. From the coast he sent a rider back to the column: they had come out to protect their caravan, their men and their goods, God had delivered them, and they should go home.[^s3-8]

The column, at that point, began to turn round. A story in which the Meccan army was already withdrawing when it was talked back into a battle it then lost does nothing for anybody's theology — it makes the victory of the seventeenth a victory over men who had not wanted to be there — and it survives for no better reason than that it happened. What stopped the turn was a debt. Some months earlier, at Nakhla, a raiding party of Muhammad's had killed a Meccan client named 'Amr ibn al-Hadrami in a month in which fighting was forbidden; the blood was never settled. Ibn Ishaq has Abu Jahl refuse the withdrawal outright: they would not go back, he said, until they had been to Badr, where they would halt three days, slaughter camels, feast, pour wine and let the Arabs hear of it, so that the Arabs would fear them for ever.[^s3-9]

That answer explains why Quraysh marched and why they marched on after the reason for marching had gone. The caravan brought them out of Mecca; what kept them on the road once it was safe was the road itself. A town that lived by moving other men's goods along a corridor eight hundred miles long could not afford to be seen chased off it, and the Arabs whose good behaviour that corridor depended on were watching.[^s3-10] Abu Jahl's three days of feasting were not bluster. They were the purpose of the expedition once the caravan was out of it.

Not everyone accepted it. At the halt where Abu Sufyan's rider reached them, al-Akhnas ibn Shariq, the Thaqafi confederate who spoke for Banu Zuhra, put it to his people that the caravan was safe, that Zuhra had no claim in the Hadrami blood, and that nothing was to be got by marching on Medina; and he took them home. Not one man of Banu Zuhra was at Badr, and al-Akhnas was afterwards reckoned the most fortunate man in Quraysh.[^s3-11] Banu 'Adi ibn Ka'b sent nobody at all, and Banu Hashim and Banu al-Muttalib came unwillingly and under pressure. The army that came over al-'Aqanqal was smaller than the army that had left Mecca, and it was already an army that had argued.

It argued once more, on the far bank. 'Utba ibn Rabi'a of 'Abd Shams was the senior man of Quraysh on the ground, over sixty, and he did not want the battle. He mounted a red camel and rode along the front of his own people telling them they would gain nothing by fighting Muhammad and his companions: if they killed him, every man among them would afterwards be looking into the face of a cousin he had killed. Better to leave him to the other Arabs. And — the offer that mattered, because it removed the last legal ground for the war — he would pay the blood-money for 'Amr ibn al-Hadrami himself, blood-money being the compensation in camels or coin that settled a killing between clans and closed the claim.[^s3-12]

He sent Hakim ibn Hizam, a nephew of Khadija and a moderate, round the chiefs with the proposal, and Hakim found them willing. Then he took it to Abu Jahl, who was oiling his coat of mail, and Abu Jahl said that 'Utba's lungs had swelled up when he saw how few the Muslims were, and that his own son was among them and he was afraid for him.

Then Abu Jahl sent for 'Amir ibn al-Hadrami, the dead man's brother, and told him his ally wanted to go home with his revenge in front of his eyes, and he had better get up and call for his dead. 'Amir got up, uncovered himself, threw dust on his head and cried out for 'Amr — the formal cry of the unavenged, which no man of standing could hear and walk away from. The proposal died there. Abu Jahl finished it by calling 'Utba a coward, and 'Utba called for a helmet, could find none in the camp large enough for his head, and bound a cloth round it instead.[^s3-13]

On the eastern side of the wadi that same evening, the Muslims came down through the gravel gap and halted short of the wells, and Hubab ibn al-Mundhir, an Ansari of Banu Salima, came to the Prophet and asked the question that made the difference. Was this a place God had ordered him to occupy, so that they might neither go forward from it nor fall back, or was it a matter of opinion and stratagem of war? It was opinion and stratagem of war. Then, said Hubab, this was not the place: take the army forward to the well nearest the enemy, camp on it, fill in the wells beyond, and dig a cistern and fill it, so that the Muslims would fight and drink and Quraysh would fight without drinking. Ibn Ishaq reports that the Prophet accepted the advice and moved the army before dark.[^s3-14]

It is the most consequential piece of ground-reading in the campaign, and the exchange is also evidence of how the men around Muhammad understood him: such a question could be asked out loud and answered without offence. What it produced was a camp astride the water. The Muslims drank, watered their camels, filled their skins, and puddled the floor of a basin a few paces across and filled that too — a dark pool of standing water two hundred paces behind where their line would stand. The wells nearest the Mecca approach were broken in and filled with sand.[^s3-15]

Quraysh, coming up the Mecca road in the dark, had to cross the ridge; the alternative was to fight for water they could not see against an enemy already standing on it. They walked their column up the gentle back of al-'Aqanqal and brought it down the slip-face, men and horses and seven hundred camels, into the dune belt at the bottom. Descending loose sand at thirty degrees is bad going for a man and worse for a horse, which cannot see its footing, breaks the crust, and slides on its hocks; a hundred horses came down that face in the dark, and their riders put them to no use next day that any source records.[^s3-16] The Meccans came off the sand onto the far bank having drunk last on the road, three-quarters of a mile from the wells, the whole Muslim army between them and a drink.

By the time the sun went down on the sixteenth of Ramadan, the Muslims had the water behind them and Quraysh had a sandhill at their backs.

[^s3-1]: For the topography I follow the descriptions in Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of the expedition to Badr, and al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, checked against the modern site. Badr lies at roughly 23° 47′ N, 38° 47′ E, some seventy miles direct from Medina and about eighty by the track, and about a hundred and seventy-five miles direct from Mecca. Yalyal is the parent drainage; its upstream reach nearer Medina is Wadi al-Safra', and the sources use the two names loosely. al-Waqidi is the fullest on the ground at Badr and is the source for most of the small circumstantial detail here; he is also the source one should be most careful with, precisely because circumstantial detail is what a good storyteller supplies. Where his topography can be checked against the site it holds up, which is a point in his favour.

The verse is the earliest layer of evidence for the battlefield's geometry and independently confirms the arrangement the narrative sources describe: the Muslims on the Medina side, Quraysh on the Mecca side, the caravan lower down toward the sea.

I have kept Aslam, whose name is the better attested of the two.
[^s3-5]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of Badr, for the beating and the rebuke; the report is also in al-Tabari, *Tarikh*, the year 2 AH, and in Ibn Kathir, *al-Bidaya wa'l-Nihaya*, iii, and Muslim, *Sahih*, Kitab al-Jihad wa'l-Siyar, preserves a related tradition on the questioning of the Badr water-carriers. That the beating is remembered at all, and remembered as a rebuke to the Muslims, is a small but useful indication that this material was not wholly smoothed. The wording of the Prophet's deduction from the camels varies: "between nine hundred and a thousand" is the common form, and al-Waqidi has a version in which the arithmetic is done for the reader.
[^s3-6]: The fullest of the chapter's disputes, and worth setting out. Four figures are in play. (i) Ibn Ishaq's, arrived at by the camel-and-loaves calculation: between nine hundred and a thousand. (ii) The round "one thousand", which is what most later writers give and what Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids), reports; it is almost certainly the Mecca muster figure rather than the field figure, and round thousands in this literature are estimates dressed as counts. (iii) al-Waqidi's clan-by-clan tallies, which are the most impressive-looking evidence and the least trustworthy, since they sum to about nine hundred and fifty only if one adopts his own reconciliations; tallies of this kind are reconstructions from genealogy, not muster rolls. (iv) A minimalist reading, which observes that the badri lists on the Muslim side are demonstrably swollen by later claimants and infers that the Meccan figure inflated in step, and would put the Meccan force nearer seven hundred. The minimalist case is respectable but it proves less than it claims: the direction of exaggeration in this literature runs toward magnifying the odds against the Muslims, so the Muslim figure of three hundred and thirteen — small, awkward, and not round — is unlikely to have been reduced, and there is no independent reason to think the Meccan figure was raised beyond what a Meccan muster could actually produce. Mecca and its confederates could field a thousand men; that they fielded between nine hundred and a thousand, less the Zuhra withdrawal, is what the earliest report says, and the only one that shows its working. The camel-count is the strongest single piece of evidence in the whole numerical question, because it is an inference from a fact a slave would have no reason to falsify, and it is not the sort of argument a later embellisher constructs. Hence "roughly nine hundred and fifty", stated as round and meant as round.
[^s3-7]: On the hundred horses: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of Badr, and Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids), give one hundred; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, has two hundred, and the higher figure is repeated by some later compilers. One hundred is preferable on two grounds: it is the earlier report, and two hundred cavalry against a force of nine hundred and fifty foot would be an extraordinary proportion for any Arabian army of the period, whereas one horse to nine or ten men is normal. The point is in any case nearly moot, since no source records the Meccan horse delivering a charge; see the note on the descent of al-'Aqanqal. For the Muslim two horses and seventy camels, see the note in the previous section. al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, on the drums, the wine and the singing-women; Ibn Ishaq preserves fragments of the satirical verse. The Muslim column, by contrast, moved without baggage, drums or women.

[^s3-9]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of Badr, for both the refusal and the three days at Badr; the same speech in al-Tabari, *Tarikh*, the year 2 AH. On the Nakhla affair and the killing of 'Amr ibn al-Hadrami in a sacred month, see Ibn Ishaq's account of the raid of 'Abdallah ibn Jahsh; the standing blood-claim it created is the single indispensable fact for understanding why the Meccan column did not go home.
[^s3-10]: The economic reading of Meccan behaviour is Watt's, in *Muhammad at Medina*, and in a different form Donner's, in "Mecca's Food Supplies and Muhammad's Boycott"; both are argued more fully in the last section of this chapter. It should be said that neither the road nor Meccan honour is a sufficient cause on its own, and that the sources give the honour more weight than the road — Abu Jahl's stated reason is what the Arabs would say, not what the caravans would cost.
[^s3-11]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of Badr, and Ibn Sa'd, *Tabaqat*, ii (the raids), on al-Akhnas and Banu Zuhra. How many men he took away is disputed: al-Waqidi's reckoning yields about a hundred, which is a plausible Zuhra contingent, while the tradition Ibn Ishaq transmits implies as many as three hundred, and the difference between the "thousand" that left Mecca and the "nine hundred and fifty" at Badr will not carry either figure. The safest statement is that Zuhra withdrew entire, that no man of them was at Badr, and that the withdrawal accounts for part but not all of the shrinkage of the column. That al-Akhnas was long remembered as the luckiest man in Quraysh suggests the number was large enough to notice.
[^s3-12]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of Badr, for 'Utba's speech from the red camel; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, is fuller and adds the offer to cover the caravan's costs as well as the blood-money. Whether the whole episode is a piece of Rabi'a-family apologia, invented or polished by 'Utba's descendants to put their ancestor on the right side of a battle he lost, deserves to be taken seriously and should be rejected. Family tradition in this literature works by making a man look brave, or wise, or generous. It does not work by having him ride down his own line arguing for retreat, get called a coward by a rival to his face, fail to find a helmet that fits, and die in a duel he had tried to avoid. The proposal makes 'Utba look weak, and the material around it is unflattering in exactly the way invented material is not. It is also structurally necessary: something has to explain why an army that had begun to turn back turned round again, and 'Utba's failed offer explains it.
[^s3-13]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of Badr, for Hakim ibn Hizam's shuttling, Abu Jahl at his mail, the taunt about 'Utba's son, and 'Amir ibn al-Hadrami's cry; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, for the helmet and the cloth. Abu Jahl's insult is given in a coarser form in some recensions than I have rendered it. 'Utba's son Abu Hudhayfa ibn 'Utba was indeed in the Muslim ranks, and 'Utba's brother Shayba and his other son al-Walid were beside him; the kinship arithmetic of that one family is the battle in miniature.
[^s3-14]: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of Badr, for the exchange, quoted here from that version; al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, has it with the cistern specified and the wells "filled in". The reply — that it was opinion and stratagem of war — is stable across the versions, and it is the load-bearing part. On whether the episode is historical: it has been suggested that Hubab's advice is a doublet, a later tradition that transferred to a named Ansari a judgement the Prophet had made himself, in the manner of other stories that supply a Companion as the human occasion for a decision. Two things tell against it. The first is that the story is theologically awkward — it has the Prophet halt in the wrong place and be corrected — and awkwardness of that kind is rarely manufactured. The second is that the tactical content is specific and correct: moving to the well nearest the enemy, disabling the rest, and building a cistern is exactly what a man who knew that ground would advise, and the arrangement is independently required by the position both armies are found in at dawn. The advice is historical in substance. Whether Hubab's precise words are his is another matter, and the wording does vary; what does not vary is the distinction he draws between revelation and judgement, which is why the exchange has been quoted so often since.
[^s3-15]: al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, on the cistern (*hawd*) and the filling of the wells. Whether the surplus wells were stopped up or merely held under guard is not certain; the verb in the reports can bear either sense, and "filled in" may be an intensification of "denied". Either way the effect on the day was the same.
[^s3-16]: The descent of al-'Aqanqal is universally reported: Ibn Ishaq (Ibn Hisham), *Sira*, the account of Badr, and al-Waqidi, *Kitab al-Maghazi*, book of Badr, both have Quraysh come down from the sandhill into the valley. That no source records a cavalry charge at Badr, in a battle where one side had a hundred horses and the other two, is among the strongest confirmations that the ground was as the sources describe it. A horse taken down a thirty-degree slip-face in the dark arrives at the bottom in no condition to be ridden at a spear-line, and the belt of soft dune sand it would have had to cross to reach one was the worst going in the valley.
